Description

Between AD 33 and 1400, the people of Europe gradually converted from a variety of other religions to Christianity. In this course, we will consider both the (scanty) evidence for pre-Christian religions and the narratives of conversion for each region of Europe in the post-Roman period (i.e. after AD 400). The focus of the course will be a critical examination of the original written sources and material remains that tell us about the pre-Christian religions of Europe, the conversion of each group, and the impact of Christianity. 

 

Learning Objectives (also known as Outcomes)

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• a knowledge of key events, people, and groups involved in the conversion of Europe to Christianity between 300 and 1500.

• the ability to evaluate and compare primary sources, both written texts and material culture, to gain information about the distant past

• the ability to use primary sources in writing about history

     - drawing inferences and information from primary sources.

     - paraphrasing and quoting fairly and accurately; quoting appropriately and contextually.

Requirements

The class will consist of a mix of lectures and discussion; if you have a question at any time during the lecture, feel free to raise your hand and ask.  If you find something confusing, probably many other people do too, and they will appreciate your question also.

 

If at any time outside of class you have questions or wish to talk to me about any aspect of the class, or about history, or about anything else, please come to my office hours, as listed above.  If these times are not convenient, I will be happy to schedule an appointment.  I am also very accessible via e-mail.

 

The course will require weekly readings and short exercises on primary sources, participation in class discussion, and a midterm and final exam, graded as follows:


Attendance 5%
Participation in discussions 8%
9 exercises (out of 13 assigned) (5% each) 45%
midterm exam 16%
final exam 26%

Attendance will be taken in class; your grade will reflect the numerical percentage of the classes you attend.  You will be allowed two free absences (i.e. if you attend all but two classes, you will still have 100%).  If you are sick, please do not come to class - let me know and your absence will be excused.  If you have another kind of excusable absence that can be documented, your absence will be excused and will NOT count as one of your two free absences.

 

Each Tuesday we will spend part of the class time discussing the assigned readings from the previous week.  These have attached written exercises, which will be due at the start of class on that Tuesday.  You are required to write most of these exercises, and thus to be able to talk about what you have written; but you should talk in class even if you have not written that week's exercise.  Participation in these discussions will count for 10% of your grade.

 

A written exercise has been assigned for 13 of the discussion section meetings (no exercise the first week, none the week of the midterm).  Each exercise is based on the assigned primary source readings for that week, and most are due at the start of class on the following Tuesday (a few are due on Thursday).  You must turn in each assignment in Canvas's "Assignments" feature, either by copying and pasting your essay or uploading a document.  You must do 9 out of the 13 exercises assigned, and you will not be allowed to turn exercises in late.  I suggest that you start writing the exercises at the start of the semester (i.e. don't put it off!), so that when unexpected events take place, you'll be caught up.

 

The exercises are short essays, which should be 300-600 words (approx. 1-2 double-spaced pages).  Less than 300 words will not count and you will receive a 0.  You should make specific reference to the relevant primary source(s), as well as to lectures or material from the textbook to provide a context for the source.  Be sure to answer the assigned question.

 

The midterm exam will consist of identifications and short answer questions.  Half of the final exam will be similar in format, covering material from the second half of the class (i.e. not comprehensive), while the other half of the final exam will consist of an essay (topic given out in advance) covering material from the entire semester.

 

It is intellectually dishonest, and against university rules, to copy information from a previous paper, from a book, or from a website, without citing your source -- this is called plagiarism. For more information, see https://wts.indiana.edu/writing-guides/plagiarism.html.  If you plagiarize, submit the same paper as someone else, or are otherwise academically dishonest, you will be reported to the Office of Student Ethics, and you will fail that assignment.  If you are in any doubt, you should cite your source.

 

Extra Credit

Extra credit of 1% on your semester average will be given for each satisfactory (B or higher) exercise turned in above the assigned 9, up to a total of four extra credit points.  Thus, if you turn in 13 satisfactory exercises, you will receive an extra 4% on your semester average.  Grades will be calculated in a variety of ways, to your best advantage.

 

There will also be possibilities for extra credit to be earned by attending lectures and conference sessions, and writing up a summary.  These will be announced via email through Canvas, and also in class.  If you attend such an event and write up a one-page, double-spaced summary of it, you will earn 2% on your semester grade.

 

You are allowed a maximum of 4% extra credit altogether.
