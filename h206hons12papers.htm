Some Guidelines for your Papers

 

 

A.  General information

 

What I want you to learn from these papers is that history is based on evidence, and that it is not permissable to make a statement about something in the past without basing it on some type of evidence from the time period.  Evidence can be written - documentary, literary, historical - or it can be material - archaeological remains, surviving buildings and works of art, etc.  All evidence requires interpretation, of course, and that is what I am asking you to do in your papers.  I am giving you a finite amount of evidence for each paper, and asking you to interpret only this evidence (and not any other) to produce a piece of historical writing.


A primary source is a text that was actually written in the time period to which it pertains.  A modern example would be a novel written in 2010, your driver's license, a train schedule, a newspaper from this year, etc.  You have been given primary source readings in association with each unit (these can be accessed by the links on the online syllabus).  Some of the readings are about the specific communities we are discussing, but some are not.  In the latter case, the readings contain information about the society that is closely related in time and space to that community, and so you are to use it as evidence for what life might have been like in each community.

 

Your other primary sources are the material remains from each community - the layout, the buildings, the material remains, the art -  as presented in class and as found on the associated websites (linked from the online syllabus).

 

The articles and chapters that have been assigned discuss issues related to the meaning and interpretation of this evidence, or else provide a discussion of a different type of evidence and show what can be done with it.  You are to use these articles to think about the assigned question, and you should certainly quote them in your papers.

 


 

B.  Requirements for the papers

 

In your papers, you must do the following five things.  Each of the following criteria is worth one letter grade.  If one of these is missing, you will be marked down a letter grade, if two are missing, you are down two letter grades, etc.

 

1) Answer the question.

Read the assigned question on the syllabus carefully. IF YOU CAN'T FIND THE QUESTION, ASK!  Most of the paper assignments involve two or more related parts. When you are writing your paper, make sure you answer all parts of the question.


You are expected to use the assigned article/chapter to help you answer the question, and to refer to it in your footnotes.

 

2) Historical context

In each paper, you should not be talking about "medieval times", but you should know something about the particular time and place that your paper is about:  6th century Italy, 12th century England, etc.

 

Look over your class notes and skim the assigned reading in the textbook to refresh your sense of the historical context. Note that the historical context should complement your analysis but should never be the focal point.

 

3) Primary and secondary sources

You must use evidence from three of the assigned primary sources.  "Using evidence" in this case means deriving information directly from a source, quoting or paraphrasing from the source, and identifying that source in a footnote.  For example:


As we walk from the church to the palace, we pass a group of Gothic tribunes, and then troops of soliders marching here and there.1

________

1 Ambrose of Milan, letter 20, "to his sister".


There is no formal footnote format required; you may simply use the reference that is provided on the syllabus.

 

Read each source carefully. Ask yourself the following questions:

 

               1) What kind of document is it (A legal code? A letter? An inventory? A sermon?)

              

               2) What is it about? Whose point of view is expressed in the document (is it biased toward one party or another? How?) What is the document's purpose?

 

               4) What kind of information can you find in it that will help you to answer the question? *** Keep in mind that even if the document does not relate specifically to the community you are writing about, it may pertain to a  community similar to it. In this case it is acceptable to make comparisons.

 

               5) Very long quotes should be avoided unless absolutely necessary. Only highlight the information that is relevant to your point and paraphrase the rest.  If you quote more than three lines, you should indent that quote and single-space it.

 

4) Specific information about the community

You must use information about the physical remains from each community (Ravenna, West Stow, etc.) as evidence in your essay.

 

Look at both the Powerpoint presentations and the webpages linked to the syllabus for information about the community.  How does this information relate specifically to the question?  How can you use specific pieces of information about the community as evidence to answer the question?

 

One problem many students have is that they simply say "Wharram Percy was a feudal village."  This is not obvious; it has to be proven.  If you say "Wharram Percy had one or two manors that were obviously larger and more imposing, but most of the village consisted of peasant households; this corresponds to what we know about feudal social organization" you are on the right track. 

 

You must be specific.  Thus, don't just say something vague about how big and impressive Chartres cathedral is; say something specific about one part of it.  You have been provided with plenty of data; you are expected to use it.

 

5) Organization and style.

One of the criteria for grading will be a well-organized and well-written essay.

 

Before you start writing, think about the connections between primary sources, communities, and the historical context in relation to the question.  The connections are there -- you just need to find them to put it all together.

 

Next, underline the passages from the primary sources that you think will help you to answer the question and choose the images that you will use to support your points. Make a short outline.

 

Your paper MUST have a clear introduction. Do not simply rephrase the question; instead, provide a short overview of what you will cover in your paper.  In the case of the first set of topics, it is entirely permissible, even encouraged, to conclude your introductory paragraph with the statement "In this paper I will. . .".  Such a statement both helps you to figure out what you are doing in this paper, and helps the reader understand what you are doing throughout the paper.  Such a statement should probably be answering the assigned questions; if what you are doing in the paper doesn't answer the questions, you're not doing the right thing.

 

The body of your paper should support your points with primary sources, material covered in class, and historical context.

 

Grammar, spelling, and punctuation will all be part of this component of the grade. 

 

If you need help with your writing, Writing Tutorial Services provides both group and individual instruction; I strongly encourage you to consult them early in the semester.
