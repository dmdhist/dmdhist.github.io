SAGA OF OLAF TRYGGVASON

from

Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla

 

Snorri Sturluson (c. 1179-1241) was a Christian scholar who lived in Iceland; he was a prominent political figure, and visited Norway and Sweden.  Iceland had converted to Christianity by the year 1000, but Snorri was deeply interested in Iceland's past, and he recorded what he claimed were legends, myths, fragments of older poems, and histories, all in Old Norse.  His other most famous work is the Poetic Edda, a compilation of Norse mythology.  Because Snorri was supported by the kings of Norway (who were trying to take over Iceland), he also recorded a lot of Norwegian history. 

 

Written in Old Norse around 1225-30, the Heimskringla  ("the disk of the world") consists of a saga about each king of Norway or Sweden, going back to the mythological prehistory of the dynasty, but really beginning with Halfdan the Black in the 9th-10th century, up to 1184.  Snorri used earlier royal sagas, some of which survive; he also had access to now-lost manuscripts, and to skaldic (i.e. orally-transmitted) poems, which he frequently quotes.  For the saga of Olaf Tryggvason, he had a now-lost Latin biography written in the 1190s.

 

While some earlier kings had become Christian, Olaf Tryggvason (known as St. Olaf) is given credit for definitively converting Norway to Christianity.  Part of his legend is that he disappeared in the middle of a sea battle and was never seen again.  As you will see in the selections given below, Snorri does not hide the fact that Olaf's efforts at conversion were controversial.

32. OLAF IS BAPTIZED.

 

While Olaf Tryggvason was staying in the Scilly Isles he heard of a certain prophet on the islands, who could tell beforehand things not yet done, and many believed that what he foretold was really fulfilled.  Olaf became curious to try this man's prophecy.  He therefore sent one of his men, who was the handsomest and strongest, clothed him magnificently, and bade him say he was the king; for Olaf was known in all countries as handsomer, nobler, and bigger than all others.  But, since he had left Garðaríki, he shortened his name to Ali, saying he was Russian.

 

When the messenger came to the prophet, and said he was the king, he got the reply, "You are not the king, but this is my advice, that you be true to your king."  And he would not say more to that man.  The messenger returned, and told Olaf, and his desire to meet the prophet was increased; and now he had no doubt of his being really a prophet.  Olaf went to see him, and, entering into conversation, asked him if he could foresee how it would go with him with regard to his kingdom, or of any other fortune he was to have.  The hermit replies with a holy prophecy:

 

"You will become a renowned king, and do celebrated deeds.  You will bring many men to faith and baptism, and you will benefit yourself in this and many others.  And so that you may have no doubts about these answers of mine, you can take this as a sign: at your ships you will meet treachery and uprising, and it will lead to a battle, and you will lose some of your troops and yourself receive a wound and because of this wound you will be at the point of death and be carried to your ship on a shield, but you will recover from this wound within seven nights and soon receive  baptism."

 

Soon afterward, Olaf went down to his ships, where he met some hostile men who wanted to kill him and his men.  A fight took place, and the result was what the hermit had predicted, that Olaf was wounded, and carried to his ship, and that his wound was healed in seven days.  Then Olaf perceived that the man had spoken truth, that he was a true prophet, wherever it was that he had his prophecy from.  Olaf went a second time to this man, discussing then many things with him, and asked particularly how he came to have such wisdom in foreseeing things that had not yet happened.  The hermit says that the Christian God himself let him know all that he wanted to know; and he tells Olaf many of God's great wonders.  In consequence of these representations Olaf agreed to receive baptism, and he and all his followers were baptized forthwith.  He remained there a long time, learned the true faith, and took away from there priests and other clerics.

47. OLAF TRYGGVASON COMES TO NORWAY.

 

Thorir sailed west to Dublin in Ireland, and inquired about Ali.  He was there with his wife's brother King Olaf Kvaran.  Thorir, who was a clever talker, got into conversation with Ali.  When they had been talking for a long time, Ali began to inquire about news from Norway, and above all of the Upland kings and great people,—which of them were alive, and what territories they now ruled.  He asked also about Jarl Haakon, and if he was much liked in the country.

 

Thorir replies, "The jarl is such a powerful man that no one dares to speak otherwise than what he wishes; but that comes from there being nobody else in the country to look to.   But, to say the truth, I know the minds of many noble people and also of the commoners, that they would much prefer and welcome a king of Harald Fairhair's lineage to come to the kingdom.  But we know of no one available, especially now that it is proved that it does not pay to fight against Jarl Haakon."

 

When they had spoken of this often, Olaf reveals to Thorir his name and lineage, and asks him his opinion, and whether he thought the farmers would take him for their king if he were to appear in Norway.  Thorir encouraged him very eagerly to this enterprise, and praised him and his talents highly.  Then Olaf's inclination to visit his patrimony became strong.  Olaf sailed accordingly, accompanied by Thorir, with five ships; first to the Hebrides, and from there to Orkney.  At that time Jarl Sigurd, Hlǫðvisson, lay in Osmundswall, in the island Rǫgnvaldsey, with one warship, on his way to Caithness.

 

Just at the same time Olaf was sailing with his fleet from the west to the islands, and sailed into the same harbour, because Pentland Firth could not be crossed at that tide.  When the king was informed that the jarl was there, he had the jarl called to talk with him; and when the jarl came to speak with the king, after a few words only had passed between them, the king says the jarl must have himself baptized, and all the people of his country also, or he should be put to death on the spot; and the king assured the jarl he would lay waste the islands with fire and sword, if the people did not become Christians.  In the position the jarl found himself, he preferred becoming Christian, and he and all who were with him were baptized.  After that the jarl swore oaths to the king, and became his man, and gave him his son, whose name was Hvelp or Hundi, as a hostage; and the king took Hvelp to Norway with him.

 

Then Olaf sails out to sea to the eastward, and made land at Morster Island, making his first landing in Norway there, and having a mass sung in his land-tent, and afterwards on the spot a church was built.  Thorir Klakka tells the king that the best plan for him would be not to make it known who he was, or to let any report about him get abroad; but to seek out the jarl as fast as possible and fall upon him by surprise.  King Olaf does so, sailing northward day and night, when wind permitted, and did not let the people of the country know who it was that was sailing in such haste.  And when he came north to Agdanes, he heard that Jarl Haakon was in the fjord, and was in disagreement with the farmers.  On hearing this, Thorir saw that things were going in a very different way from what he expected; for after the battle with the Jomsvikings everyone in Norway had been wholehearted supporters of Jarl Haakon because of the victory he had won, and of the peace and security he had given to the country.  But now it had turned out badly, in that a great leader has come to the country at a time when the farmers are at odds with the jarl.

53. OLAF BAPTIZES THE REGION OF VIK.

 

When Harald Gormsson, king of Denmark, had adopted Christianity, he sent a message over all his kingdom that all people should be baptized, and turn to the true faith.  He himself followed his message, and used force and punishment where nothing else would work.  He sent two jarls, Urguthrjot and Brimilskjar, to Norway, to preach Christianity there.  In Vik, which stood directly under the king's power, this was successful, and many of the country folk were baptized.  But after King Harald's death his son Svein Forkbeard soon went raising in Saxony, Frisia, and at last in England, and the people of Norway who had taken up Christianity turned back to heathen sacrifices, just as before, the same as those practised by people in the north of the country.

 

But when Olaf Tryggvason had become king in Norway, he stayed for a large part of the summer (A.D. 996) in Vik.  Many of his kindsmen came to him there, and some of his relations by marriage, and also many who had been great friends of his father; so that he was welcomed with the greatest affection.  Olaf called to speak with him his mother's brothers, his stepfather Lodin, and his brothers-in-law Thorgeir and Hyrning, then put before them with the greatest earnestness the proposal that they themselves should back him and then support him with all their might in his desire to establish the preaching of Christianity all over his kingdom, saying that he shall achieve the Christianisation of everywhere in Norway or die in the attempt. "I shall make you all great and powerful men, for I trust you best on account of our kinship or other ties."

 

They all agreed to do what he asked, and to support him in what he wanted to do, together with everyone who was willing to follow their lead.  King Olaf immediately announced to the public that he intends to preach Christianity to all the people in his kingdom.  The first to agree to accept this proposal were those who had already given him their promise.  These being the most powerful among the people assembled, the others followed their example, and all the inhabitants of the east part of Vik were baptized. The king then went to the north part of Vik and instructed everyone to accept Christianity; and those who opposed him he punished severely, killing some, having others maimed, and driving some into banishment.  So it came about that that in the kingdom which his father King Trvggvi had ruled, and also that of his kinsman Harald Grenske, all the people accepted Christianity; and during that summer and the following winter all Vik became fully Christian.

61. ROGALAND BAPTIZED.

 

When King Olaf came to Rogaland, he immediately summoned the people to a Thing[meeting]; and when the farmers received the summons for the Thing, they assembled in great numbers well-armed.  After they had come together, they resolved to choose three men, the best speakers in their group, to reply to King Olaf, and speak in opposition against the king; and especially that they would not accept wrongful laws, even if the are introduced by the king.

 

But when the farmers came to the Thing, and the Thing was in session, then King Olaf arose, stood up, and at first spoke kindly to the farners.  Yet it was apparent from what he said that he was determined that they should accept Christianity, inviting them with fine words, but adding finally for the benefit of those who objected and would not accede to his request, that they would have to face anger and punishments and harsh terms from him wherever he was able to bring it about.  

 

When he had ended his speech, then rose the farmer who was considered the most eloquent, and who had been appointed as the first who should reply to King Olaf.  But when he tried to begin his speech, he was attacked by such a coughing and shortness of breath that he could not bring out a word, and had to sit down again.  Then another farmer stood up, determined not to let the answers fail to be presented, although it had gone so badly with the previous man.  But he stammered so much that he could not get a word out, and all present began to laugh, so the farmer sat down again.  And now the third stood up to make a speech in opposition to King Olaf, but when he began to speak he became so hoarse and husky in his throat, that nobody could hear a word he said, and he also had to sit down.  There was none of the farmers now to speak against the king, and as nobody answered him there was no opposition; and it came to this, that all agreed to what the king had proposed.  All the people of the Thing accordingly were baptized before the Thing was disbanded.

65. THE THING IN THRONDHJEM.

 

In the summer, King Olaf collected a great army from the east of the country, and sailed with it north to Nidaros in the Throndhjem district.  From there he sent a summons all around the fjord, calling the people of eight different districts to a Thing; but the farmers changed the Thing-summons into a war-token; and called together all men, free and unfree, in all the Throndhjem district.  

 

And when the king came to the Thing, the whole people came fully armed.  After the Thing was seated, the king spoke to the people and asked them to adopt Christianity.  But he had only spoken a short time when the farmers called out to him to be silent, or they would attack him and drive him away. "We did this," they said, "to Haakon foster-son of Athelstan, when he made the same request of us, and we pay no more regard to you than to him."

 

And when King Olaf saw how incensed the farmers were, and that they had such a war force that he could make no resistance, he changed his tack and turned to agreement with the farmers, saying, "I want us to agree together, as we have previously settled things between us.  I want to go to where you hold your greatest worship and see your practices there. Then we shall hold discussions about the practices, which ones we want to have, and then all come to an agreement about it."

 

And as the king spoke mildly to the farmers, their attitude was softened, and afterwards the whole talk went pleasantly and in a conciliatory fashion, and it was decided in the end that there should be a midsummer festival in at Mærin, and all the leaders and rich farmers would attend, as the custom was.  King Olaf would also be there.

68. OF THE THING IN THRONDHJEM.

 

King Olaf went with his whole force into the Throndhjem district; and when he came to Mærin, all the leaders of the Throndhjem people who were most opposed to Christianity were assembled, and had with them all the great farmers who had before made sacrifice at that place. There was thus a large multitude of farmers there, as there had been at the Frosta-Thing.

 

Then the king let the people be summoned to the Thing, and both parties went fully armed.  And when the Thing was seated, the king spoke, preaching Christianity to the people.  Jarnskegge replied on the part of the farmers, and says that the will of the farmers is now, as formerly, that the king should not break their laws. "We desire, king," he says, "that you should offer sacrifices, as other kings have done before you."

 

All the farmers applauded his speech loudly, saying they wanted to have all things according to what Jarnskegge said.  Then the king said he would go into the temple of their gods with them, and see their rituals when they offer sacrifice.  The farmers were pleased at this, and both parties went to the temple.

 

69. THE THRONDHJEM PEOPLE BAPTIZED.

 

Now King Olaf entered into the temple with a small number of his men and some of the farmers; and when the king came to where their gods were, there sat Thor, the most dignified of all the gods, adorned with gold and silver.  King Olaf lifted up his gold-inlaid axe which he carried in his hand, and struck Thor so that the image rolled down from its pedestal.  Then the king's men leapt forward threw down all the gods from their pedestals; and while the king was in the temple, Jarnskegge was killed outside of the temple doors, and the king's men did it.  When the king came forth out of the temple he offered the farmers two alternatives, the one that all should now receive Christianity, or as an alternative that they should fight against him.

 

But as Jarnskegge was killed, there was no leader in the farmers' army to raise the banner against King Olaf; so they took the other condition, to surrender to the king's will and obey his order. Then King Olaf had all the people present baptized, and took hostages from them for their remaining true to Christianity; and he sent his men round to every district, and no one in the Throndhjem country now opposed Christianity, but all the people were baptized.

73. THANGBRAND THE PRIEST GOES TO ICELAND (AD 997)

 

When King Olaf Trygvason had been king in Norway for two years, there was a Saxon priest in his house who was called Thangbrand.  He was a very violent man and a fighter, but a good scholar and a valiant man.  The king did not want to have him in his house upon account of his unruly behavior; but gave him the mission to go to Iceland, and bring that land to the Christian faith.

 

He was provided with a merchant ship: and there is this to be told of his voyage.  He landed first in Iceland in the eastern fjords in the southern Alptfjord, and passed the winter in the house of Hall of Sida.  Thangbrand preached Christianity in Iceland, and because of what he said, Hall and all his household, and many other leading men, had themselves baptized; but there were many more who spoke against it.  Thorvald Veile and the poet Veterlide composed insulting verses about Thangbrand, and he killed them both.  Thangbrand stayed two years in Iceland, and was the slayer of three men before he went away.
