Description
After the "fall of the Roman Empire", western Europe and the Mediterranean experienced invasion, plague, religious conversion, and other upheavals that shaped entirely new political, social, and cultural systems. In the year 500, new kingdoms and identities were still very closely associated with the Roman world that continued in the eastern Mediterranean, but by the year 1000, western Europe was divided into many different political units, with two dominant religions, Christianity and Islam, no longer unified with the eastern and southern Mediterranean areas. Europe in 1000 contained many of the political, cultural, religious, ethnic, and linguistic boundaries that we know today, and thus the Early Middle Ages can be regarded as the period in which some of the foundations of modern European society were put into place. We will be examining the different ways that Roman, Germanic, Christian, and Islamic traditions interacted to produce this new world.

We will begin by briefly considering the "fall of Rome," and will then look at the political, cultural, and religious institutions that arose in the wake of the dissolution of a dominant European power. We will end around the year 1050, because after 1050 Europe again experienced a large number of new trends and transformations. Throughout the semester, we will be examining the different ways that Roman, Germanic, Christian, and Islamic traditions interacted to produce a new Europe.

Learning Outcomes
The goals of this class are that students will develop the following:
- knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, religions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of western Europe between 500 and 1000
- awareness that social, cultural, and political trends are complex, and related to each other, and discussion of how, for example, theological disputes shaped and were shaped by their social and political contexts
- appreciation of some of the major artistic, architectural, and literary trends throughout this period, and how they were related to political and social stimuli
- facility in reading and understanding fascinating written primary sources in a variety of genres (diplomatic correspondence, legal code, charters, documents, personal correspondence, historiography)
- the ability to write history by deploying relevant evidence from primary sources to explain life and culture in the past
- the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally

WRITING IS THINKING. The point of the papers is to have you actually construct historical arguments based on lengthy primary sources - you will have to dig for information to use, and you will have to do this on paper in class. Descriptions of the papers can be found here.

Each paper is based on one of the book-length primary source readings for the class. You must acquire a print copy of each book, which will be used in class, and YOU MUST USE THE VERSION OF THE TEXT THAT IS ASSIGNED ON THIS SYLLABUS - IF YOU DO NOT, YOU WILL AUTOMATICALLY FAIL THE PAPER. You are expected to have read each book BEFORE the Workday for that paper.

Each paper must be turned in on Canvas by midnight on the day that it is due. Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (up to two weeks after the original due date). You do not have to use that option; remember that if a paper is late, it is not guaranteed to be graded before the next one is due. For the AI policy for these papers, see the detailed paper instructions.

On four days we will have a debate in which members from the class participate. You will be assigned to a team of 4-5 students for one debate. ASSIGNMENTS WILL BE MADE AT THE THIRD CLASS MEETING. You are required to meet with your debate team at least once before the debate. You will be graded individually on your participation, and each person will have ample opportunity to participate. A website has been created for each debate, linked to the online syllabus, that contains the debate topic, readings (or links to readings), images, and other materials. Further instructions for the debates can be found here.

The midterm and final exams will be in-class essay exams. They will be open-note tests; you may use any printed materials that you like, but you may not use any computer or electronic devices. It behooves you, therefore, to come to the exams with good notes, whether based on class meetings, readings, or other materials. You may also take as much time as you want to complete these exams. Because this form of test is often difficult for people who haven't taken one before, a study guide will be made available two weeks before the midterm.
