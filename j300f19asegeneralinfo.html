Description

When the Roman legions pulled out of Britain in the early fifth century, the island entered a "dark age", during which groups of people from the continent, known to us collectively as Anglo-Saxons, entered Britain and became its rulers.  In this course, we will examine the history and culture of Anglo-Saxon England from 400 to 1066. We will consider a variety of issues such as the history of Christianity in England, social and political development, contacts with other islands and the continent, the repeated waves of invaders, the gradual unification of the island, and finally the the Norman conquest of 1066.  We will read extensively in both primary and secondary sources, and we will also examine archaeological material in detail. 


One of the expectations of a seminar is that students will learn how to discuss history orally, and to write as historians write history.  In addition to discussing the history of Anglo-Saxon England, we will be discussing historial methodology and historical writing, both through discussion of your own research projects and through discussion of books and articles written by modern historians about Anglo-Saxon England.  We will pay particular attention to the way that scholars construct their arguments using primary sources of various kinds.


In this writing-intensive seminar, you will read a lot, write short response papers about readings, produce a 5-7-page paper about secondary sources, write a book report, and write a longer research paper.


Learning outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• knowledge of the history of Anglo-Saxon England.

• the ability to evaluate and compare scholarly arguments.

• the ability to produce a historical argument based on different types of appropriately selected primary and/or secondary sources.

• the ability to present analysis and research in written form.

• the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally.

Requirements

This course will be taught as a seminar; that is, classes will be discussion based, and students should expect to participate actively in debate and discussion.  It is crucial that you do the readings for each class before you come to class, otherwise very little will make sense.  


A CASE Intensive Writing class has the following requirements:  write at least 5000 words, not counting revisions, response statements, reflection papers, annotated bibliographies, etc.  At least one paper must be redrafted in light of the professor's commentary.


A paper written in 12-point font, double-spaced with 1" margins is about 300 words/page.  Thus, 5000 words is about 17 pages of writing.  Your words will be counted, to make sure that you have written enough for IW credit!


Attendance and participation in discussion                                                15%

Attendance will be taken in every class.  Participation means having done the assigned reading for the day, having formed opinions about it based on the set response question for the day, and taking part in discussion.  By Oct. 21 you will be given a grade on your performance up to that point; that grade will not factor into your final grade, but is an indication of where you currently stand.


For 19 of the class meetings there is an assigned reading response question. SIX OF THEM ARE REQUIRED; you must complete a total of 13 of them (including the required ones).  You can answer the prompt in paragraphs or in note-style form, but you must answer them completely - which means approximately two full paragraphs' worth of information, in the Assignments section of Canvas. THEY MUST BE COMPLETED BEFORE THE START OF CLASS ON THE DAY FOR WHICH THEY ARE ASSIGNED.  These will be graded from 0-2 points.  2 points means that it is clear that you read the full assignment and thought about it; 0.5 point means that it's not clear that you read it all, or that you gave it much thought.  I suggest that you plan your schedule carefully, including the other paper assignments and book reports, so that your workload is evenly distributed; I also suggest that you do the earlier ones in case you are sick or have some other emergency.  Your grade for this component of the class will be based on your turning in the correct number of responses, and performing satisfactorily on a majority of them.


Each student will write a 5-7 page paper (12-point font, double spaced, 1" margins) on the following topic:  "Which type of evidence, archaeological or textual, provides more convincing material for reconstructing early Anglo-Saxon history?  How do scholars use the two types of evidence:  do they always use both appropriately?"  You are expected to make reference, with footnotes, to all the materials assigned for class, but you are not expected to do any outside reading for this paper.  This paper is due in class on Oct. 2.


This paper will be returned to you, with comments and a grade, on Oct. 7.  You are required to modify your paper on the basis of these comments, and submit the revised version and the original version (with the comments) on Oct. 21.  If you have addressed all of the comments appropriately, then the grade that you get for the original version will be an A, and the grade on the revised version will be whatever you have earned.  If you do not address the comments in the revised version, then the original grade will stand and the revised paper will get its own grade.


Each student will read, summarize, and briefly present to the class one book report on a scholarly book. We will assign these books reports after you decide on your paper topic, as your book will form part of the bibliography for your term paper.  The oral book report should take no more than 8 minutes (practice!  and be concise).  In it you should briefly explain (a) what the subject and thesis of the book are; (b) whether you can tell if the author is arguing against other theories; (c) what primary sources the author uses in the book; and (d) whether you found it informative and convincing.  Your written report should be three full pages (i.e. more than 2.75 pages) long, and should summarize the book and also answer the questions from the oral report. Book reports must be turned in to Canvas on the day that the report is presented in class.


Your term paper must be 9-12 pages (12-point font, double spaced, 1" margins).    A list of suggested topics can be found at the end of the syllabus; you must choose one of these topics.   Once you have been assigned a topic, you must meet with Prof. Deliyannis outside of class, within two weeks, either in office hours or by appointment.   She will provide you with a bibliography that includes primary and secondary sources; you must read all of these items for your paper.


On Nov. 20 you must turn in an annotated bibliography for the paper.  For each item on your bibliography, you must include brief notes on what each item is about, and how you think it will help you to write your paper.  Naturally, this means that you must have read or at least skimmed the items!


During the last two weeks of class (after Thanksgiving), each of you will give a short (8-10 minute) presentation of the material that will be covered in your paper.  Your presentation will be graded on how clearly you explain the topic of your paper, whether you use appropriate visual aids (i.e. powerpoint), whether you stick to the time limit, and how well you answer questions.  Dates will be randomly assigned once topics have been assigned, so that we can have coherent sessions.

Academic Integrity

As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code). Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution. Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty. When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced. Any ideas or materials taken from another source for either written or oral use must be fully acknowledged. All suspected violations of the Code will be reported to the Dean of Students and handled according to University policies. Sanctions for academic misconduct may include a failing grade on the assignment, reduction in your final course grade, and a failing grade in the course, among other possibilities. If you are unsure about the expectations for completing an assignment or taking a test or exam, be sure to seek clarification from your instructor in advance.


Term paper topics

You will select a paper topics from the list found here; each student will write on a different topic.  You must choose one of these topics, unless you have a solid background in medieval studies and are willing to do research on your own.  If there is a particular topic that you want to pursue that is not listed here, you must discuss it with me by the second week of classes.


When your topics has been assigned, Prof. Deliyannis will provide you with at least one primary source and at least six secondary sources that must be used in your final paper.  At least one of the secondary sources will be a monograph (book by one author), which you will use for your book report.  In other words, she will do the research for you, but you must do the reading, thinking, and writing based on the sources you have been given.


A good research paper uses primary sources and secondary sources that have been written about the topic.  While reading the secondary sources, you may well find references to more primary sources.  Some of these may not be translated into English; if you have questions, ask!


If you discover additional sources, you are welcome to use those also, but you must use the ones you have been assigned.


The thesis of your paper will be an answer to the question that is the topic.
