Gateway course - Rationales

What would a gateway (of any kind) do?

Gateway courses can help smooth students’ transitions into the upper-level courses in the department and introduce students to the skills of the historian at a basic level. The principle behind any gateway would not be “once and done,” that is, that students would be introduced to skills and approaches and then be left to their own devices. Instead, the gateway course would be the first step in establishing a “spiral curriculum,” that is, a curriculum in which students would constantly return to approaches and skills they had already been introduced to, but that greater facility and depth and complexity would be required of them at each iteration. [1] Such a course would prepare students for higher impact practices at the upper levels in our courses, particularly but not limited to undergraduate research/a true capstone course or project.

A major issue for us is getting our students to see how all of their work fits together, that is, to integrate their knowledge. This has been one of the most important themes in the liberal arts education reform movement. [2] Right now our students’ knowledge is fragmented. A gateway course wouldn’t be able to do all the work that we need to have done to produce students whose knowledge of history is integrated—meeting the dean’s comments about wanting to produce self-actualizing students (a concept drawing on the work of Marcia Baxter-Magolda who has written extensively about self-authorship) [3]—but it would be a place to start.

There are implications to the decision to put in a gateway course. In a sense whatever kind of gateway we install will be cementing in place a first step up the stairway of the major. The later steps will have to connect with that course of those courses and we’ll have to think hard about how they do. In our upper-level courses, for instance, teachers might have to ask the history majors in their classes what gateway they took (if we have several), or perhaps begin with a metacognitive reflection on what they’d learned in the gateway and how that might apply to the class they were about to take. We will need to make more explicit the ways in which the things going on in their classes connect to what was happening in the gateway course. There are, of course, different options for how a gateway course would be handled and there are potential objections to the institution of such a course, which we will discuss below.

 

a.  A single gateway course
Who is doing it: Michigan, Wisconsin, Nebraska, Ohio State, Illinois [variously titled “The Historian’s Craft,” “Introduction to History,” “Historical Methods,” and other like titles.]
Discussion: We already have in progress approval to add a course with a system number, H270 "What is History?” to the IUB catalogue. Whether we go with H270 or design a course with a new number, such a course might be taught by anyone in the department interested in doing so and as a single course might make more predictable what our majors had been exposed to. If every student had to take a single gateway course, it would give them a common experience as well, and perhaps an esprit de corps. Peter Felten in his recent SoTL talk (November 13, 2014) noted that a forthcoming study done at Hamilton College suggests that what really makes a difference in college education is a close relationship with one teacher and having three close friends; this would offer the potential for students to develop such friendships around an academic subject. We have been lamenting the absence among our students of being in it “together” and hence their lack of interest in the Primary Source or a History Club or even Phi Alpha Theta. It is also clear that the graduate students form bonds in H601 across their fields of study. Because such a course would have to be created, owned, and staffed by the department as a whole (see below), it might also enhance esprit de corps among faculty, something we have been struggling to do in the last few years. Teaching one section of such a course each semester would not create undue problems for staffing our courses and, if there were sections, might provide work for graduate students.

The conventional wisdom is that students hate required courses because they are required courses. Whether or not this is true, the content of the course would present some challenges. If the course were taught with a particular subject matter, we would have the same response we already get to the seminars—students might not want to take a course with that subject matter. But if we taught it solely from a history skills perspective, we would run into other problems. We ourselves were drawn into history by our love of a particular content. Furthermore, it is problematic to divorce thinking about a particular historical content and the problems it presents from the skills (or ways of thinking) necessary to make sense of it. [4] As Martin and Monte-Sano point out: [5]

While a focus on ways of thinking may evoke ideas that we favor skills over content, we do not subscribe to the false dichotomy of teaching historical content or teaching historical skills. Historical content is not just what is already known, the substantive ideas and information of the discipline, but also how we know the past. Teaching for historical thinking means students must work with particulars and specifies about the past while reading and analyzing texts, and investigating historical questions.

That’s what happens with composition courses, where students report overwhelmingly that they don’t see the connection between these courses and the writing they do in their disciplines. Faculty complain about this and eventually cease to expect such application.

Furthermore, if we have a single gateway course, the department as a whole will have to create it, own it, staff it and reference it in their other teaching. Staffing it probably means a section each semester. Obviously, it could not and should not be the purview of a single faculty member. The content would have to be relatively stable and changes made only by consultation within the department. We haven’t done this sort of thing in recent memory and we haven’t asked faculty to teach within these sorts of constraints.
Finally, all the things said below in favor of not having a gateway course would apply here: it would mean some students might have to take a course for which they would not get history credit, so they might be forced to take more than the minimum number of courses for the major; such a course might come relatively late in their pursuit of the major, and so forth.

 

b. A set of gateway courses at the 200 level, whether seminar or lecture.
Who is doing it: UCLA (16 courses entitled Introduction to Historical Practice); Duke (17 “gateway seminars” [equivalent to our 200-level seminars] on restricted topics).
Discussion: Having a set of gateway courses would get around the problem of content/skills by allowing for a set of courses committed to teaching historical skills each with a specific historical content. These courses might have an agreed upon set of skills to be worked on with a menu of means to teach them. Each instructor would be committed to an active/engaged approach to teaching history, that is, students would actually have to utilize historical skills in some active form: for example, students might do a source analysis, critique a book or article, design a poster, write a mini-research paper—there are a lot of possibilities. These courses would be open to other students, who might choose them to fulfill general education requirements and would present the possibility of recruiting some of these non-majors, but they would offer the majors a platform to develop skills to be deployed in upper-level courses.

There are some real issues about the form that such a course would take. Would these courses be seminars and have the consequent tight focus that our seminars have tended to have? The Duke gateway seminars do exactly this—with titles like “China at War” (which begins in the late 19th century). UCLA in its “Introduction to Historical Practice” course has different course numbers for the different fields, so there is History 97D Introduction to Historical Practice: Variable topics in US History and so forth. This also suggests a fairly tight focus. On the other hand, one might allow such a course to range widely in a given topic area to permit students to work with material they found intrinsically interesting.

Because there would be options, students might be less resistant to a new requirement. The risk here, however, is fragmentation. If there is more than one gateway course being taught, how will this help people teaching upper-level classes? They might not know which course a student had taken and there might be students in a given upper-level course who had taken different gateways and therefore were bringing different things into the course. Students also wouldn’t have the social benefits of a shared cohort experience. While one could argue that this is always true now, it is what makes teaching our majors more complicated. There is also a risk in that courses become routinized over time and may be taught by people who didn’t originate the course. Activities that were originally designed to promote learning can devolve into a checklist of things to do, without much understanding of the reasons why the course is the way it is. A regular approval process might address this concern, but even at Alverno College, which has been doing extraordinary things in relation to student learning for more than forty years now, keeping on track requires a commitment to regular maintenance by their departments (which tend to be small—only about five people). We are far larger than that and do not, as yet, have that kind of culture of cooperation.

Finally, a system of new courses may present staffing issues. These might be met by having graduate students teach these courses or by having a faculty member teach one of these courses and supervise or coordinate graduate students teaching others, but designing new courses and revising old courses to new purposes would require faculty labor.

 

c. Add no requirement and continue the way we do now. (Implication: The gateway resides at the upper level if there is any gateway at all.)
Who is doing it: Kentucky (300-level seminars); Notre Dame (300-level single course); Minnesota (single 300-level course); Purdue, Northwestern, UNC, University of Texas, Yale (no requirement); Penn State (300-level seminar requirement)

Discussion: There was some sentiment in the feedback the UAC got from the faculty to have as few requirements as possible. The fear is that if we institute new requirements or prerequisites, we will lose majors. It might be argued that we will lose majors anyway, as this is a national trend not just in history, but across the humanities, but the risk that we will lose students we might otherwise recruit is real, if difficult to evaluate, as is the quality of the students thus recruited. Leaving the entry end of the major open allows students to drift in. Having a requirement when they decide to major may discourage them. Furthermore, because students often declare after taking a number of history courses, the gateway course might not come to them until near the end of their programs. It might be argued that J300 in some senses serves as a gateway for many of our students, and at some institutions, the curriculum functions that way. A gateway course, probably at the 200 level, would impose a burden on some of our students, who will come in with four 100- or 200-level courses already, whether because they come in from Ivy Tech or bring in AP or ACP courses. They would be forced to take a course which didn’t count for credit toward the major (although it would probably count toward their degrees; there is, however, a ceiling on the number of courses a student can take in a single department for degree credit). For all these reasons, gateways are problematic in our sort of system.

On the other hand, not having a gateway puts considerable pressure on courses at the 300 level to teach basic skills rather than helping students deepen and develop skills they already have. Furthermore, while students do sometimes enter the major after taking a number of courses, a gateway course would probably ensure that they had been introduced to historical thinking before they start taking the seminars. Many of our students already take more history than is required—30 hours is only about one year’s worth of courses--so taking a course that was required but didn’t count toward the major would not necessarily be a burden, at least for those students. In addition, some students arrive from Ivy Tech (for instance) planning to study history, but find it too different from what they experienced before (which is basically an extension of high school stand-and-memorize history). A gateway might ease that transition for them.
