Course description

 

When the Roman legions pulled out of Britain in the early fifth century, the island entered a "dark age", during which groups of people from the continent, known to us collectively as Anglo-Saxons, entered Britain and became its rulers.  In this course, we will examine the history and culture of Anglo-Saxon England from 400 to 1100.  We will consider a variety of issues such as the history of Christianity in England, social and political development, contacts with other islands and the continent, the repeated waves of invaders, the gradual unification of the island, and finally the the Norman conquest of 1066. We will read extensively in both primary and secondary sources, and we will also examine archaeological material in detail. 

 

One of the expectations of a seminar is that history students will learn how to write as historians write history.  In addition to discussing the history of Anglo-Saxon England, we will be discussing historial methodology and historical writing, both through discussion of your own research projects and through discussion of books and articles written by modern historians about Anglo-Saxon England.  We will pay particular attention to the way that scholars use the available primary sources to construct an argument.

 

 

Course goals

 

This course has two goals:

 

1)  to introduce students to an in-depth study of the history of Anglo-Saxon England, both primary sources and modern debates.

 

2)  to introduce students to historical research and writing.

Requirements

 

This course will be taught as a seminar; that is, classes will be discussion based, and students should expect to participate actively in debate and discussion.  It is crucial that you do the readings for each class before you come to class, otherwise very little will make sense.   The major requirement of the course will be a research paper on a topic of the student's choice, which will also be presented orally to the class at the end of the semester.

 

Attendance and participation in discussion                                           15%

Response questions to readings  (must do 13 out of 19)                 10%

Short (5-7 pg) paper due Oct. 4                                                               10%

Book report - written (3 pg) and oral                                                       10%

Term paper bibliography (due Sept. 20)                                                   5%

Term paper annotated bibliography (due Nov. 1)                                   5%

Term paper draft (due Nov. 29)                                                                  5%

Term paper presentation                                                                          15%

Term paper (12-14 pg), due Dec. 13                                                       25%

 

Attendance will be taken in every class.  Participation means having done the assigned reading for the day, having formed opinions about it based on the set response question for the day, and taking part in discussion.  By Oct. 6 you will be given a grade on your performance up to that point; that grade will not factor into your final grade, but is an indication of where you currently stand.

 

For 19 of the class meetings there is an assigned reading response question.  FOUR OF THEM ARE REQUIRED; you must complete a total of 13 of them (including the required ones).  You are to answer this question or questions in note-style form (i.e. you do not need complete sentences, but you must answer them completely) in the Assignments2 section of Oncourse.  THEY MUST BE ANSWERED BEFORE THE START OF CLASS ON THE DAY FOR WHICH THEY ARE ASSIGNED.  These will be graded with check/check-minus/F.   I suggest that you plan your schedule carefully, including the other paper assignments and book reports, so that your workload is evenly distributed; I also suggest that you do the earlier ones in case you are sick or have some other emergency.  Your grade for this component of the class will be based on your turning in the correct number of papers, and performing satisfactorily on a majority of them.

 

Each student will write a 5-7 page paper (12-point font, double spaced, 1" margins) on the following topic:  "Which type of evidence, archaeological or textual, provides more convincing material for reconstructing early Anglo-Saxon history?  How do scholars use the two types of evidence:  do they always use both appropriately?"  You are expected to make reference, with footnotes, to all the materials assigned for class, but you are not expected to do any outside reading for this paper.  This paper is due in class on Oct. 4.

 

Each student will read, summarize, and briefly present to the class one book report on a scholarly book. We will assign these books reports after you decide on your paper topic, so that your book can form part of the bibliography for your term paper.  The books are listed by date on the syllabus, and can be found on the online bibliography. Your book oral report should take no more than 8 minutes (practice!  and be concise).  In it you should briefly explain (a) what the subject and thesis of the book are; (b) whether you can tell if the author is arguing against other theories; (c) what primary sources the author uses in the book; and (d) whether you found it informative and convincing.  Your written report should be three pages (i.e. more than 2.75 pages) long, and should summarize the book and also answer the questions from the oral report. 

 

Your research paper should be 12-14 pages (12-point font, double spaced, 1" margins), and will be on a topic of your choice.  A list of suggested topics can be found at the end of the syllabus, but you may write about anything that interests you.  Paper topics will be discussed in class on Sept. 6, and must be officially declared in writing on September 13. 

 

On Sept. 22 you must turn in a preliminary bibliography for the paper.  You are expected to have done bibliographic research in the databases listed below on this syllabus, and your bibliography should include both primary and secondary sources that you plan to use.

 

On Nov. 1 you must turn in an annotated bibliography for the paper.  This may be quite different from your preliminary bibliography; in addition to primary and secondary sources, you should include brief notes on what each item is about, and how you think it will help you to write your paper.  Naturally, this means that you must have read or at least skimmed the items!

 

A draft of your paper is due in class time on Nov. 22 (you may turn it in via Dropbox in Oncourse).  You should consider that this is a substantially complete version of your paper.  It will not be sufficient to turn in an introductory paragraph plus notes.   Drafts will be returned on Dec. 1, with comments for improvement, and you will be expected to actually improve your paper, according to suggestions, before you turn in the final paper by December 13 at 4:45 pm.

 

During the last two weeks of class, you will give a short (8-10 minute) presentation of the material that will be covered in your paper.  Your presentation will be graded on how clearly you explain the topic of your paper, whether you use appropriate visual aids (diagrams, transparencies, etc.), whether you stick to the time limit, and how well you answer questions.

 

 

Suggested Term paper topics

 

A good term paper should use primary sources, plus any secondary sources that have been written about the question.  While reading the secondary sources, you may well find references to more primary sources.  Some of these may not be translated into English; if you have questions, ask!

 

One way to configure a question is to ask:

 

         What evidence is there for ______ in the written sources of the Anglo-Saxon period?

 

You may break it down so that you just consider the early Anglo-Saxon period, or just the late.  You could, depending on the topic and the text, use just one text or author (Bede, say, or the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle).  The subject could be a topic such as queens, agriculture, houses, church architecture, weapons, knowledge of Greek, Roman literature, the aristocracy, peasants, Jews, the plague, relations with the continent, etc. etc. etc.  For many of these topics, you certainly can use archaeological evidence also, which is another kind of primary source.

 

Another kind of question could be more general (but there you need to make sure that you can use primary sources):

 

         What was the Anglo-Saxons' attitude to Rome?

         What was the role of slaves in Anglo-Saxon England?

         How were the Anglo-Saxons viewed on the continent?

         What do we really know about Anglo-Saxon paganism?

         Why did Old English come to be the language of Britain - or did it?

         What can we learn by looking at who became archbishop of Canterbury throughout the Anglo-Saxon period?

         What was a Bretwalda (or was there any such thing?)?

 

Or, you can focus on a particular object or person:

 

         What does the Franks Casket mean?

         When was Beowulf written, and why does it matter?

         Why did Wilfrid inspire contradictory opinions?

         What do we learn about Alfred from his own writings?

         What was Emma's role in the Anglo-Saxon succession crises of the 11th century?

 

Or, you can pick something else that interests you, or ask me to help you figure something out.
