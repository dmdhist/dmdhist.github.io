Cairo (Fustat and al-Qahira), an Islamic City
AH 359-596 (AD 970-1200)

The area now encompassing the city of Cairo was developed by the Muslim rulers of Egypt in several stages. Immediately after the Arab conquest in 641, the first governor 'Amr ibn al'As founded a settlement called al-Fustat (later also known as Misr and Old Cairo). There had been a small fortress, called Babilyun, on the Nile, close to the point where it splits into branches, and Fustat was located just to the north of this area, which remained the focus of Christian (Coptic) life in the city.

Fustat was originally a military garrison, and it was located not just at a strategic point on the Nile, but also at the origin of a Roman canal that linked the Nile with the Red Sea. Fustat contained the first mosque in Egypt, the Mosque of Amr, founded when the town was established in 641. The town gradually became a city, and served as the capital of the Egyptian province throughout most of the medieval period. In 750, when the Abbasids took over the caliphate, they established a new settlement just to the northeast, called al-Askar, and when an Abbasid governor, Ibn Tulun, took control of Egypt in 868, he developed a new settlement, al-Qata'i, 2 miles north of Fustat. Al-Qata'i included a palace, a mosque (the Ibn Tulun mosque), government buildings, a racetrack, all provisioned with water from the Nile by an aqueduct.

As Fustat grew from a military garrison town to a city, its population increased dramatically, and by the end of the 9th century, the region (including al-Askar and al-Qata'i) is estimated to have had a population of around 200,000-250,000 people.

In 969, Egypt was invaded by an upcoming power from Tunisia in North Africa, a dynasty that became known as the Fatimids, because their rulers claimed descent from Fatima, daughter of Mohammed, and used the title caliph. Their conquering general, Jahwar, immediately founded a new city 1.75 miles to the northeast of al-Qata'i, which contained a palace, a central square, gardens, and a mosque (now called the al-Azhar mosque), surrounded by a wall. In 973 the caliph, al-Mu'izz, moved his court to the new city, which he called Al-Qahira ("the victorious"). Al-Qahira became the center of the royal court and the government, while Fustat remained the economic and population center, including shops, manufacturing, and shipping. Two more large mosques were built to the north of the palace (al-Hakim [990s] and al-Aqmar [1120s] mosques) new stone wall, with imposing gates, was built around al-Qahira in the 1080s.

Originally, al-Qahira was the city of the caliphal court, and throughout the Fatimid period it remained the city of the elite, with Fustat as the home of the rest of the population. By 1168, the region of Fustat and Al-Qahira may have had a population of 300,000 or more.

By the middle of the 12th century, the Fatimid dynasty was faltering; many of the caliphs were children, and the nobility fought for control of the office of vizier, the chief executive of the state. European Crusaders, whose kingdom was based in Jerusalem, attempted to conquer Egypt in order to expand their territory. During one such invasion, in 1168, the Fatimid vizier, Shawar, supposedly ordered all citizens of Fustat to shelter in Cairo, and then deliberately set Fustat on fire. In fact, recent archaeological evidence shows that this may be a later legend, that Fustat had been in decline for decades before 1168, but continued to be occupied afterward.

The Bab al-Futuh gate of al-Qahira, built 1087

Soon after this, Shawar was murdered, the viziership of Egypt was taken over by a Kurdish general from Syria named Salah al-Din (Saladin). When the last Fatimid caliph, al-Adid, died in 1171, Saladin proclaimed himself sultan, under the authority of the caliph of Baghdad.

Starting in 1176, Saladin commissioned his general Qaraqush to build a massive citadel at a rocky prominence between al-Qata'i and al-Qahira, and to surround the entire area, five miles from northeast to southwest, with a wall. Saladin died in 1193, but his successors in Egypt continued to govern from the greater Cairo area, and the city continued to be one of the largest in the Mediterranean for the rest of the Middle Ages.
