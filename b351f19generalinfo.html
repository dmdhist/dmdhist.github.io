Description

The Early Middle Ages (c. 500-1000 AD) was a time of dramatic cultural, political, and social change. During these five hundred years, Europe experienced invasion, conversion, and other upheavals that overturned the old Roman imperial order and shaped entirely new systems. By 1000, Europe contained many of the political, cultural, religious, ethnic, and linguistic boundaries that we know today, and thus the Early Middle Ages can be regarded as the period in which the foundations of modern western society were put into place. We will begin by briefly considering the "fall of Rome," and will then look at the political, cultural, and religious institutions that arose in the wake of the dissolution of a dominant European power. We will end around the year 1050, because after 1050 Europe again experienced a large number of new trends and transformations. Throughout the semester, we will be examining the different ways that Roman, Germanic, Christian, and Islamic traditions interacted to produce a new Europe.

Attendance will be taken in each class. You will be allowed two 'free' absences. If you are sick or have a valid emergency, let me know (email is fine) and your absence will be excused (i.e. not count as one of your two 'free' absences).

Class meetings will consist of lecture and discussion. Readings from the textbook are assigned in order to provide background and supplementary material to what happens in class. Discussion, group activities, debates, and other types of exercises will take place at various points in the semester, and you will be expected to be prepared for these.

Descriptions of the papers, including the topic on which you must write, can be found here. Each paper is based on one of the book-length primary source readings for the class. You must read each book, as well as any relevant textbook readings, by the time you write your paper. Each paper must be turned in in class on the day that it is due, and we will discuss the papers in class. Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (up to two weeks after the original due date). You do not have to use that option; remember that if a paper is late, it is not guaranteed to be graded before the next one is due.

On four days we will have a debate in which members from the class participate. You will be assigned to a team of 3-4 students for one debate. ASSIGNMENTS WILL BE MADE AT THE FOURTH CLASS MEETING. You are required to meet with your debate team at least once before the debate; the instructor will help to facilitate these meetings. You will be graded individually on your participation, and each person will have ample opportunity to participate. A website has been created for each debate, linked to the online syllabus, that contains the debate topic, readings (or links to readings), images, and other materials. Further instructions for the debates can be found here.

The midterm and final exams will be a combination of short identifications and essays (they may also include map sections). They will be open-note tests; you may NOT however use books or photocopies. It behooves you, therefore, to attend class and take good notes; also to take good notes on readings and other materials. Because this form of test is often difficult for people who haven't taken one before, a study guide will be made available two weeks before the midterm.

There will probably be opportunities for extra credit during the semester; usually this involves attending a lecture and writing a one-page summary of it. These will be announced as the dates are publicized. Note that attending a lecture or session (and writing a summary) will earn you 2% on your semester average; I will give each person a maximum of 4% extra credit earned in this fashion. Summaries must be turned in one week after the event.

Learning Outcomes
A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:
- knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, religions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of western Europe between 500 and 1000.
- awareness that social, cultural, and political trends are related to each other, and discussion of how, for example, theological disputes shaped and were shaped by their social and political contexts
- knowledge of some of the major artistic, architectural, and literary trends throughout this period, and how they were related to political and social stimuli
- facility in reading and understanding written primary sources in a variety of genres (diplomatic correspondence, legal code, charters, personal correspondence, historiography)
- the ability to deploy relevant evidence from primary sources, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, to construct historical arguments about life and culture in the past
- the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally
