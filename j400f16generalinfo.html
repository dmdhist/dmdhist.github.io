Description

Rome. Brasilia. Abu Dhabi. Chang'an. Accra. Springfield. Even Bloomington! Cities that are designated as centers of political units can be called "capital cities". Some were cities before they became capitals; others were planned from scratch to be the practical and symbolic showpieces of their state, nation, or empire. In this course, we will examine capital cities across time and space, as representative of the intersection of urban history and political identity. From Constantinople to Washington DC, we will consider what functions take place in capital cities, what groups of people live and work there, and how such cities are physically laid out to express the aspirations and ideals of the political unit they control. Students will read theoretical articles about capital cities, and will examine several case studies. Then, each student will complete a research project on any historical aspect of a capital city of his/her choice. 


Grades will be based on attendance and participation in discussion; on short assignments designed to facilitate the production of the final project; on the final project itself (which can be a research paper, a video, exhibit, film, or other format); and on a presentation of the research project in one of the last weeks of the semester.



Learning outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• knowledge of theoretical and methodological understandings of capital cities.

• the ability to frame a research question, identify primary sources that could be used to answer it, as well as secondary sources to provide a historiographical framework

• the ability to produce an argument based on different types of appropriately selected primary and/or secondary sources.

• the ability to present analysis and research in a well-crafted and coherent form, as a research paper, video, exhibit, film, or other long format.

• the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally.

Attendance will be taken in every class. Participation means having done the assigned reading for the day, having formed opinions about it based on the set discussion question for the day, and taking part in discussion. Your grade for this component will be based on your attendance percentage, modified by your participation. Thus, if you have 100% attendance but never talk, you will earn a B-; if you have 87% attendance but contribute frequently and usefully to the class, you will earn an A- or A, and so on.

For six classes there is a short written assignment based on the readings. You should upload these to Canvas Assignments in the appropriate place, BEFORE CLASS on the day they are due. These will be graded with check/check-minus/F. Your grade for this component of the class will be based on your turning in the correct number of papers, and performing satisfactorily on a majority of them.

Each student will present one book report on a scholarly book. The book will be something off your bibliography for your research project. Your book report should take no more than 10 minutes (practice! and be concise). In it you should briefly explain (a) what the subject and thesis of the book are; (b) what primary sources the author uses in the book; and (c) whether you found it informative and convincing. THERE IS NO WRITTEN COMPONENT TO THIS ASSIGNMENT.

Your research project will be on a topic of your choice. You are REQUIRED to discuss your topic and format with me, in office hours or by appointment, before the date that it is due; research topics and formats must be officially declared in writing via Canvas on Sept. 6 (just a brief one-sentence statement about what you are going to research and what the final format will be).

A preliminary bibliography is due on Canvas on Sept. 20. A preliminary thesis sentence (which presumes that you have already read many of the items on your bibliography) is due on Canvas on Oct. 25.

A detailed outline of your paper is due on Canvas on Nov. 8. This means a section-by-section explanation of your thesis, of how the paper will be laid out, what arguments and evidence will be given in each section. Rather like a rough draft, but you don't actually have to have written it, you have to know what you are going to write.

During the last two weeks of class, each student will give a short (10-12 minute) presentation of the material that will be covered in his/her paper. The dates will be assigned based on the topics of the papers. It is strongly suggested that your paper be finished by the date of your presentation. Your presentation will be graded on how clearly you explain the topic of your paper, whether you use appropriate visual aids (powerpoint or similar), whether you stick to the time limit, and how well you answer questions.

Your research project might be a 14-20-page paper (double-spaced, 12-pt font, 1" margins). Or, if you have the skills to produce another type of project in which you display and argument and the fruits of your research (a video, exhibit, etc.), you may take that option. In either case, all notes and bibliography will use Chicago Manual of Style for references (you can access it through IUCAT). Your research project is due on Canvas on Tuesday, December 13 at 5:00 pm.
