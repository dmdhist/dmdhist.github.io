Description

"The pagans did not know they were pagans until the Christians told them they were."
- Henry Chadwick, Augustine on Pagans and Christians (1985), p. 9.

Between AD 33 and 1400, the people of Europe gradually converted from a variety of other religions to Christianity. In this course, we will consider the (scanty) evidence for pre-Christian religions and the narratives of conversion for each region of Europe, focusing on the post-Roman period (i.e. after AD 400). We will critically read both primary sources and secondary literature, and we will also consider archaeological evidence, in order to understand what can be learned about these topics. 

One of the expectations of a seminar is that students will learn how to write as historians write history. In addition to discussing the topic of pagans and Christians, we will be discussing historial methodology and historical writing, both through discussion of your own research projects and through discussion of books and articles written by modern historians about the topic. We will pay particular attention to the way that scholars use the available primary sources to construct an argument.

In this writing-intensive seminar, you will read a lot, write short response papers about readings, produce a 5-7-page paper about secondary sources, write a book report, and write a longer research paper.

Learning outcomes
A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:
• a knowledge of key events, people, and groups involved in the conversion of Europe to Christianity between 300 and 1500.
• the ability to evaluate and compare scholarly arguments.
• the ability to produce a historical argument based on different types of appropriately selected primary and/or secondary sources.
• the ability to present analysis and research in written form.
• the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally.

This course will be taught as a seminar; that is, classes will be discussion based, and students should expect to participate actively in debate and discussion. The class is listed as HD "Hybrid online;" most of our meetings will take place synchronously at the assigned time, but there are a couple of weeks that may be asynchronous. On zoom, we will have both all-class discussions and use breakout rooms. It is expected that you will have your camera turned on in this class; if there is a reason that you cannot turn your camera on, please email me or talk to me privately about it.  

It is crucial that you do the readings for each class before you come to class, otherwise very little will make sense.

A CASE Intensive Writing class has the following requirements:  write at least 5000 words, not counting revisions, response statements, reflection papers, annotated bibliographies, etc.  At least one paper must be redrafted in light of the professor's commentary.

All writing assignments should be submitted using 12-point font, double-spaced with 1" margins, no extra space between paragraphs. Doing this results in about 300 words/page. Thus, 5000 words is about 17 pages of writing. Your words will be counted, to make sure that you have written enough for IW credit. Using extra-wide margins, large font, extra spacing between paragraphs, etc. will not fool anyone.

Attendance will be taken each class.   However, I recognize that, given disease, isolation, connectivity issues, etc. it might not be possible for you to attend every class.   Therefore, this part of the grade will be based both on the percentage of your attendance and also on your participation in discussions, extra meetings with me outside of class, or other contributions that you make to the learning process for this class.   Participation means having done the assigned reading for the day, having formed opinions about it based on the set response question for the day, and taking part in discussion.   If you are sick, have another kind of emergency, or have internet problems during class, please send me an email and your attendance will be excused.   You will only have access to a recorded class session if you have a valid excuse.

Starting on Feb. 3 there is an assigned reading response question.   There at a total of nine; you must complete a total of seven of them (you should not do a reading response on the day you give your book report, and then you may also choose to skip one other).   You are to answer this question or questions in note-style form (i.e. you do not need complete sentences, but you must answer them completely) in the Assignments section of Canvas.   THEY MUST BE ANSWERED BEFORE THE START OF CLASS ON THE DAY FOR WHICH THEY ARE ASSIGNED. Each student will write a 5-7 page paper on the following topic: "Are we able to learn more about 'pagan' religions from texts, or from archaeology and material culture?"   You are expected to make reference, with footnotes, to all the materials assigned for class, but you are not expected to do any outside reading for this paper.   This paper is due in class on Feb. 24.

This paper will be returned to you, with comments and a grade, on Mar. 3.   You are required to modify your paper on the basis of these comments, and submit the revised version and the original version (with the comments) on Mar. 17.   If you have addressed all of the comments appropriately, then the grade that you get for the original version will be an A, and the grade on the revised version will be whatever you have earned.   If you do not address the comments in the revised version, then the original grade will stand and the revised paper will get its own grade.

Each student will read, summarize, and briefly present to the class one book report on a scholarly book. We will assign these books reports after you decide on your paper topic, as your book will form part of the bibliography for your term paper.  The oral book report should take no more than 8 minutes (practice!  and be concise).  In it you should briefly explain (a) what the subject and thesis of the book are; (b) whether you can tell if the author is arguing against other theories; (c) what primary sources the author uses in the book; and (d) whether you found it informative and convincing.  Your written report should be three full pages (i.e. more than 2.75 pages) long, and should summarize the book and also answer the questions from the oral report. Book reports must be turned in to Canvas on the day that the report is presented in class.

Your term paper must be 9-12 pages long.    A list of suggested topics can be found at the end of the syllabus; you must choose one of these topics.   Once you have been assigned a topic, you must meet with Prof. Deliyannis outside of class, within two weeks, either in office hours or by appointment.   She will provide you with a bibliography that includes primary and secondary sources; you must read all of these items for your paper.

On ____ you must turn in an annotated bibliography for the paper.  For each item on your bibliography, you must include brief notes on what each item is about, and how you think it will help you to write your paper.  Naturally, this means that you must have read or at least skimmed the items!

During the last two weeks of class, each of you will give a short (8-10 minute) presentation of the material that will be covered in your paper.  Your presentation will be graded on how clearly you explain the topic of your paper, whether you use appropriate visual aids (i.e. powerpoint), whether you stick to the time limit, and how well you answer questions.  Dates will be randomly assigned once topics have been assigned, so that we can have coherent sessions.

Academic Integrity
As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code). Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution. Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty. When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced.

In a writing class, it is particularly important that you understand both why and how you must take responsibility for your own work. Any ideas or materials taken from another source for either written or oral use must be fully acknowledged in footnotes or endnotes. All suspected violations of the Code will be reported to the Dean of Students and handled according to University policies. Sanctions for academic misconduct may include a failing grade on the assignment, reduction in your final course grade, and a failing grade in the course, among other possibilities. If you are unsure about the expectations for completing an assignment, be sure to seek clarification from Prof. Deliyannis in advance.

Term paper topics
You will select one of the topics listed below; each student will write on a different topic. You are required to choose one of these topics, unless you have a solid background in medieval studies and are willing to do research on your own. If there is a particular topic that you want to pursue that is not listed here, you must discuss it with Prof. Deliyannis by the second week of classes.

When your topic has been assigned, Prof. Deliyannis will provide you with at least one primary source and at least six secondary sources that must be used in your final paper. At least one of the secondary sources will be a monograph (book by one author), which you will use for your book report. In other words, she will do the research for you, but you must do the reading, thinking, and writing based on the sources you have been given.

A good research paper uses primary sources and secondary sources that have been written about the topic. While reading the secondary sources, you may well find references to more primary sources. Some of these may not be translated into English; if you have questions, ask!

If you discover additional sources, you are welcome to use those also, but you must use the ones you have been assigned.

The thesis of your paper will be an answer to the question that is the topic.

Material culture
Why were pagan symbols and stories illustrated on he Gosforth Cross? (England/Vikings)
Was the "Zbruch/Zbrucz Idol" a pagan religious object? (Poland)
Was the person buried in the Sutton Hoo Mound 1 a Christian? (England)
Why did Christians use pagan motifs in early Christian art? (late Roman empire)
What was the message of the images on the Franks Casket? (England)
What was the so-called "whetstone" or "scepter" from Sutton Hoo Mound 1? (England)
Is there archaeological evidence for pagan temples in England? (England)

Saints
Who was the real St. Patrick? (Ireland)
Was St. Bridgit originally a Celtic goddess? (Ireland)
Who killed St. Boniface, and why? (Germany)
Was St. Leoba the only female missionary in the early Middle Ages? (Germany)

Conversions
What is the evidence for the Khazar conversion to Judaism? (Russia)
Why does the date of King Clovis' conversion to Christianity matter? (France)
Was the conversion of the Lithuania motivated by religious or political goals? (Lithuania)
Do texts written by Christian authors adequately describe "pagan rebellions" in the face of Christian conversion? (all Europe)
Why did Charlemagne decide to forcibly convert the Saxons to Christianity? (France/Germany)
How do queens such as Gisela, Anna, and Dobrawa compare to their earlier predecessors? (Germany, Poland, Russia)
Why does the conversion of Vladimir of Rus have so many different explanations? (Russia)
Was the conversion of Ireland led by bishops or monks? (Ireland)
What was Pope Gregory the Great's motivation for sending missionaries to England? (England)
Was the Emperor Constantine's battlefield conversion genuine? (Roman empire)
What was at stake in the conflict between Roman and Irish missionaries in England? (England)

Pagan religion
Do we have enough evidence to define a god such as Svarog? (Slavs)
Does Adam of Bremen's descriptions of pagans have corroborating evidence? (Scandinavia)
Is Beowulf a Christian or a pagan text? (England)
Can we learn about paganism from the letters that popes wrote to England and Bulgaria?
What was the difference between pagan magic and Christian miracles? (all Europe)
What archaeological evidence is there for pagan temples in Scandinavia? (Scandinavia)
Does archaeology provide evidence for Irish paganism? (Ireland)
What did Charlemagne think pagan religion was? (France/Germany)

Miscellaneous
Why were pagan Viking attacks on Christians framed as a "punishment from God"? (Vikings)
How did conversion to Christianity influence ideas about marriage? (Bulgaria, England)
Why did the Visigothic church and king try to forcibly convert Jews to Christianity? (Spain)
How were crusades against pagans different from other crusades, and how were they the same? (Baltic)
What happened to the Roman Christians in Britain? (England)
Were stories about the destruction of pagan temples modelled on the Bible? (all Europe)
