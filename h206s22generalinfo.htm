Course Description

The European Middle Ages spans more than a thousand years, from approximately 400 to 1500 AD, and includes the many different political, cultural, and ethnic communities of Europe and the Mediterranean. There was not one medieval civilization, but many medieval civilizations, related in some ways to each other, but distinct and constantly changing. This class will be an introduction to the history of the Middle Ages through its culture and ways of life.  

Because the period to be covered is so vast, we will focus our attention on seven communities that are representative of some aspects of medieval history. We will look at villages and cathedral towns, manor houses and cities, and for each, we will consider who lived in the community, what activities they took part in, what the community looked like physically, and what aspects of medieval life, politics, and culture are represented there.

Desired Learning Outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:
• knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, and ideas that shaped the cultures of Europe between 300 and 1500.
• facility in reading and understanding primary sources, including both texts and material culture
• the ability to deploy relevant evidence, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, including:
     - drawing inferences from primary sources and relating that information to broad course themes
     - quoting appropriately and contextually
     - using evidence from primary sources to answer particular questions about life and culture in the past

Structure

The class will consist primarily of lectures, but I I encourage, and even require (see below) you to ask questions.   If you find something confusing, probably many other people do too, and they will appreciate your question also.

If at any time outside of class you have questions or wish to talk to me about any aspect of the class, or about history, or about anything else, please come to my office hours, as listed above.    If these times are not convenient, I will be happy to schedule an appointment; I can meet in-person or on zoom.   I am also very accessible via e-mail.

Course requirements

                      5%       Attendance at lecture
  5%       Question group (see below)
75%       Five short papers/exercises  (15% each)
15%       Final exam (in-person)
100%       TOTAL

Attendance will be taken at some point during each class; your attendance will be worth 5% of your final grade.  You will be allowed two 'free' absences - no excuse needed, and will not count against your attendance percentage.  If you are sick or have a valid emergency, email to let Prof. Deliyannis know and your absence will be excused (i.e. not count as one of your two 'free' absences).

In the third week of the semester, each of you will be assigned to a group made up of five or six people.  Each group will be assigned one class day. On your assigned day, you must sit in the front row of the classroom, and at some point during the lecture you must ask a question.   The question can be one of clarification, a request for further information, a question about something you read and didn't understand, or anything that relates in some way to the lecture.  If you ask your question, you will receive an A for this component of the course; if you do not ask a question, you will earn an F.   If you have to miss your assigned day for any reason, email me and we will reschedule you.  Please feel free to ask questions when it is not your turn, but this way everyone is guaranteed the opportunity to ask at least one question.

There are seven short (750-1000 words) papers assigned for this class; you must write five of them. THE FIRST PAPER, ON RAVENNA, IS REQUIRED. Then, of the remaining six papers, you must write four more of them. You may choose the topics that most interest you, or the ones that fit best with your schedule. All of the papers have two possible topics, you may write whichever you like. THE FINAL EXAM WILL COVER MATERIAL FROM ALL UNITS, EVEN THE ONES FOR WHICH YOU DO NOT WRITE THE PAPERS.

For each paper, an essay question or questions have been assigned.  Guidelines for the papers can be found at this link; these are quite complicated and require you to do many things, so please read them carefully.   Papers are due at 1:15 pm on the day they are due.  You must upload an electronic version (.doc, .docx, or .rtf; no .pdf) of your paper to the correct entry on Canvas Assignments.  If you send it late, you must email it to Prof. Deliyannis, who will then open Canvas so that you can submit it there.  Late papers will be marked down one-half letter grade for every 24-hour period that they are late (this includes weekend days); after eight days, they will not be accepted.

You never know what crisis will befall you during the semester; it is strongly suggested that you turn in the first five papers rather than waiting until the end of the semester.  I cannot tell you how many students have waited until the end of the semester to write papers, only to discover that they had more to do at the end of the semester than they had realized.

There will be a final in-person essay exam during the assigned final exam period for this class.  It will cover the primary source readings, terms highlighted during the lectures, and the communities that are the focus of the class.  A study guide will be given out in the last week of class. 

Extra Credit

If you choose to write more than five papers, we will either drop the lowest paper grade, or you will be given extra credit of up to 2% on your semester average for the extra paper.  You will receive 1% extra for a B or B+ paper, and 2% for an A- or A paper. We will calculate your grade to your best advantage.

There may be other opportunities to earn extra credit during the semester, by attending lectures held on campus or, possibly, on Zoom. These will be announced via Announcements on Canvas, and also in class.  If you attend such an event and write up a one-page, double-spaced summary of it, you will earn 2% on your semester grade.

You are allowed a maximum of 4% extra credit altogether.

Academic Integrity and Inclusion

As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code). Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution. Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty. When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced.

In particular, plagiarism includes copying information from a previous paper, from a book, or from a website, without citing your source. For more information, see https://wts.indiana.edu/writing-guides/plagiarism.html.  If you plagiarize, submit the same paper as someone else, or are otherwise academically dishonest, you will be reported to the Office of Student Ethics, and you will fail that assignment.  If you are in any doubt, you should cite your source.
