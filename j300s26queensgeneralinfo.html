Description
In the Middle Ages, some women exercised a surprising amount of authority - surprising to us, and sometimes surprising to their contemporaries.  In this class, we will consider queens and empresses in Europe and the Mediterranean from the late Roman empire to the fourteenth century.  We will examine sources for ruling women:  how they were described (and by whom), how they were represented in art, and what is said about them in legal and religious texts.   We will discuss the way they achieved political power, what kinds of authority they could exercise, who they married, what behaviors were expected of them, and how their personalities impacted their regions.

One of the expectations of a Writing-Intensive seminar is that students will learn how to write as historians write history.  In addition to examining royal women, we will be discussing historical methodology and historical writing, both through through discussion of books and articles written by modern historians about the topic, and in your term papers.  We will pay particular attention to the way that scholars use the available primary sources to construct an argument.

In this writing-intensive seminar, you will read a lot, write short response papers about readings, produce a 5-7-page paper that is re-written after feedback, do a book report that is both oral and written, and write a longer term paper on a topic that fits the description of the class.

Learning outcomes
A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

▪ a knowledge of the history of Europe and the Mediterranean between 300 and 1500, and of the roles played by queens and empresses.
▪ the ability to evaluate and compare scholarly arguments.
▪ the ability to produce a historical argument based on different types of appropriately selected primary and/or secondary sources.
▪ the ability to present analysis and research in written form.
▪ the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally.

Requirements
This course will be taught as a seminar; that is, classes will be discussion based, and students should expect to participate actively in debate and discussion.  It is crucial that you do the readings for each class before you come to class, otherwise very little will make sense.  

A CASE Intensive Writing class has the following requirements:  write at least 5000 words, not counting revisions, response statements, reflection papers, annotated bibliographies, etc.  At least one paper must be redrafted in light of the professor's commentary.

In the light of recent advances in AI, particularly related to writing, the instructor reserves the right to change any assignments on this syllabus.  The purpose of a writing class is to have you think about how historians write, and to analyze primary and secondary sources for history to produce narrative and historical argument.  The garbage produced by ChatGPT is bad writing and bad history, so it is expected that you will not use it; if you do, you will be reported for academic misconduct.  If need be, we will do writing in-class on paper.

All writing assignments should be submitted using 12-point font, double-spaced with 1" margins, no extra space between paragraphs.  Doing this results in about 300 words/page.  Thus, 5000 words is about 17 pages of writing.  Your words will be counted, to make sure that you have written enough for IW credit.   Using extra-wide margins, large font, extra spacing between paragraphs, etc. will not fool anyone.

Attendance will be taken each class.  Participation means having done the assigned reading for the day, having formed opinions about it based on the set response question for the day, and taking part in discussion.  You are allowed two FREE ABSENCES - i.e. they will not count against your attendance grade.  If you are sick or have another kind of emergency, please send me an email and your absence will be excused (i.e. not be counted as one of your free absences).

Starting Jan. 21, there is an assigned reading response question.  There are a total of 14, of which the first four are required; you must complete a total of 10 of them (you should not do a reading response on the day you give your book report, and you may also choose to skip three others).  The purpose of these responses is to ensure that you have read the assigned readings and are ready to discuss them. If you submit one, you will be expected to participate in the discussion on that day.  You are to answer this question or questions in note-style form (i.e. you do not need complete sentences, but you must answer them completely) in the Assignments section of Canvas.    THEY MUST BE ANSWERED BEFORE THE START OF CLASS ON THE DAY FOR WHICH THEY ARE ASSIGNED.  These will be graded from 0-2 points.  2 points means that it is clear that you read the full reading and thought about it; 0.5 point means that it's not clear that you read it all, or that you gave it much thought.  I suggest that you plan your schedule carefully, including the other paper assignments and book reports, so that your workload is evenly distributed; I also suggest that you do the earlier ones in case you are sick or have some other emergency.  Your grade for this component of the class will be based on your turning in the correct number of responses, and performing satisfactorily on a majority of them.

Each student will write a 5-7 page paper on the following topic:  "How do primary sources from the late Roman Empire and early Middle Ages describe queens? What problems are there with these sources, and how do modern historians and novelists deal with these problems?"  You are required to use AT LEAST 10 QUOTES from all the materials assigned for class (referenced, with footnotes), but you are not expected to do any outside reading for this paper.  This paper is due on Canvas at the start of class on Feb. 23.

This paper will be returned to you, with comments and a grade, on Mar. 2.  You are required to modify your paper on the basis of these comments, and submit the revised version and the original version (with the comments) on Mar. 11.   If you have addressed all of the comments appropriately, then the grade that you get for the original version will be an A, and the grade on the revised version will be whatever you have earned.  If you do not address the comments in the revised version, then the original grade will stand and the revised paper will get its own grade. 

Each student will read, summarize, and briefly present to the class one book report on a scholarly book.  We will assign these books reports after you decide on your paper topic, as your book will form part of the bibliography for your term paper.  Your book oral report should take between 5 and 8 minutes (practice!  and be concise).  In it you should briefly explain (a) what the subject and thesis of the book are; (b) what the chapter organization is, what is in each chapter; (c) whether you can tell if the author is arguing against other theories; (d) what primary sources the author uses in the book; and (e) whether you found it informative and convincing.  Your written report should be three full pages (i.e. more than 2.75 pages) long, and should discuss the same topics as the oral report.  (just as a note: AI does a terrible job with these book reports, sometimes describing a different book altogether. DO YOUR OWN WORK!)     Written book reports must be turned in to Canvas on the day that they are presented in class.

Your term paper should be 9-12 pages long.  A description of the paper can be found at the end of the syllabus.

On Apr. 8 you must turn in an annotated bibliography for the paper.  For each item on your bibliography, you must include brief notes on what each item is about, and how you think it will help you to write your paper.  Naturally, this means that you must have read or at least skimmed the items!

During the last two weeks of class, each student will give an 8-10 minute presentation of the material that will be covered in the term  paper.  Your presentation will be graded on how clearly you explain the topic of your paper, whether you use appropriate visual aids (i.e. powerpoint), whether you stick to the time limit, and how well you answer questions.  Dates will be assigned once topics have been chosen, so that we can have coherent sessions.

Academic Integrity and Inclusion
As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code).  Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution.  Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty.  When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced.  Ignorance of what constitutes academic misconduct or plagiarism is not a valid excuse.  All suspected violations of the Code will be reported to the Office of Student Conduct and handled according to University policies.  Sanctions for academic misconduct may include a failing grade on the assignment, reduction in your final course grade, and a failing grade in the course, among other possibilities.  If you are unsure about the expectations for completing an assignment, be sure to seek clarification from Prof. Deliyannis in advance.

In a writing class, it is particularly important that you understand both why and how you must take responsibility for your own work.  Any ideas or materials taken from another source for either written or oral use must be fully acknowledged in footnotes or endnotes.  Your use of anyone else's (or any AI system's) words, graphic images, calculations, or ideas should be properly cited.  

AI generators/programs such as ChatGPT, Grammarly, QuillBot, Spinbot, Dall-E, etc. should not be used for any work for this class without explicit permission of the instructor and appropriate attribution.  AI text generators should not be used for creating or revising drafts, editing your work, or reviewing a peer's work.  The use of generative AI platforms will be considered misconduct and will be reported to the Dean of Students (Office of Student Conduct) and handled according to University policies.

If you are experiencing bias in the classroom on the part of any other member, please contact Prof. Deliyannis immediately. Any act of discrimination or harassment can be reported to the university, details can be found here, see the section "Additional Contacts" at the bottom of that page.

When your topic has been assigned, Prof. Deliyannis will provide you with a bibliography consisting of at least one primary source and at least six secondary sources that must be used in your final paper.  At least one of the secondary sources will be a monograph (book by one author), which you will use for your book report.  In other words, she will do the research for you, but you must do the reading, thinking, and writing based on the sources you have been given.  You are required to cite all of the sources on your bibliography in your paper.

A good research paper uses primary sources and secondary sources that have been written about the topic.  While reading the secondary sources, you may well find references to more primary sources.  Some of these may not be translated into English; if you have questions, ask!

If you discover additional sources, you are welcome to use those also, but you must use the ones you have been assigned.

Your paper might be framed in the following way:  in the primary and secondary sources, the most important characteristic of this ruler is as _____.  You might choose one of the following roles:

- wife
- regent
- mother - ruler
- patron (of monasteries, art, architecture)
- saint
- jezebel
- something else?
