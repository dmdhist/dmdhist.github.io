Course Description

The Middle Ages spans more than a thousand years, from approximately 400 to 1500 AD, and includes the many different political, cultural, and ethnic communities of Europe and the Mediterranean.  There was not one medieval civilization, but many medieval civilizations, related in some ways to each other, but distinct and constantly changing.  This class will be an introduction to the history of the Middle Ages through its culture and ways of life.  

Because the period to be covered is so vast, we will focus our attention on six moments in time and space that are representative of some of the communities of the Middle Ages.  We will look at villages and cathedral towns, monasteries and manor houses and cities, and for each, we will consider who lived in the community, what activities they took part in, what the community looked like physically, and what aspects of medieval life, politics, and culture are represented there.

Learning Objectives

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• knowledge of key events, people, and groups that have shaped the history of Europe between 300 and 1500.

• comprehension of how primary sources, including both texts and material culture, are used to construct history.

• the ability to communicate historical arguments, and to deploy relevant evidence, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, including:

     - drawing inferences from primary sources and relating that information to broad course themes

     - paraphrasing fairly and accurately; quoting appropriately and contextually

     - connecting the evidence from the sources to the thesis of the paper

Structure

The class will consist primarily of lectures; if you have a question at any time during the lecture, feel free to raise your hand and ask.  If you find something confusing, probably many other people do too, and they will appreciate your question also.

If at any time outside of class you have questions or wish to talk to me about any aspect of the class, please come to my office hours, as listed above.  If these times are not convenient, I will be happy to schedule an appointment.  I am also very accessible via e-mail.
 


Course requirements

                5%       Attendance at lecture

                5%       Question group (see below)

              40%       Four short papers/exercises  (10% each)

              20%       Midterm exam

              30%       Final exam

           100%       TOTAL

Attendance will be taken in lecture; your attendance will be worth 5% of your final grade.  You will be allowed two 'free' absences.  If you are sick or have a valid emergency, email to let Prof. Deliyannis know and your absence will be excused (i.e. not count as one of your two 'free' absences).

In the third week of the semester, each of you will be assigned to a group made up of five or six people.  Each group will be assigned one class day.  On your assigned day, you will sit in the front row of the classroom.  At some point during the lecture you must ask a question.  The question can be one of clarification, a request for further information, a question about something you read and didn't understand, or anything that relates in some way to the lecture.  If you ask your question, you will receive an A for this component of the course; if you do not ask a question (or if you miss your assigned day), you will earn an F.  You should certainly feel free to ask questions when it is not your turn to sit in the front; but this way everyone is guaranteed the opportunity to ask at least one question.

The first assignment, which is required, for the Ravenna unit, is an exercise that you can find on Oncourse under Assignments, called "Ravenna exercise".  You must complete this exercise by Jan. 30 at 11:15 am; exercises will not be accepted after this time.  This exercise consists of a worksheet that will use information from lectures, the textbook, the primary sources, and the city of Ravenna; it is preparation for writing the papers that are assigned for the other five units.


The short (750-1000 words) papers on each of the other five units will be due in class the day following completion of the unit.  Of the five papers, you must write three of them. You may choose the topics that most interest you, or the ones that fit best with your schedule.  Most of the papers have two possible topics, you may write whichever you like.  KEEP IN MIND THAT THE MIDTERM AND FINAL EXAMS WILL COVER MATERIAL FROM ALL UNITS, EVEN THOSE FOR WHICH YOU SKIP THE PAPER; YOU ARE STILL RESPONSIBLE FOR THE MATERIAL.

For each paper, an essay question has been assigned; you should answer the question specifically by making references to the texts that are the outside readings (and refer to them by name in your paper).  Guidelines for the papers can be found at this link.  

Papers are due at 11:15 am on the day they are due.  You must upload an electronic version of your essay to the correct entry on Oncourse Assignments.  If you need to send it late, you must email it to Prof. Deliyannis.  Late papers will be marked down one letter grade for every 24-hour period that they are late (this includes weekend days); after four days, they will not be accepted, period.

You never know what crisis will befall you during the semester; it is strongly suggested that you turn in the first three papers rather than waiting until the end of the semester.  I cannot tell you how many students have waited until the end of the semester to write papers, only to discover that Little 500 is more than they thought it would be . . .

The midterm and final exam will consist of a combination of short answer identifications and short essay questions.  Questions will address material covered in lectures and the textbook, and also specific questions about the primary source readings.  A study guide with information for the midterm will be posted two weeks prior to the exam, and the Course Assistants will run review sessions before both the midterm and the final exams. 



Extra Credit

If you choose to turn in more than three papers, extra credit of up to 2% on your semester average will be given for each extra paper.  You will receive 1% extra for a B or B+ paper, and 2% for an A- or A paper.  We will calculate your grade to your best advantage; if it turns out that the best thing is to drop the lowest paper grade, we will do that.

There will be other opportunities to earn extra credit during the semester, by attending lectures held at IU.  These will be announced via email through Oncourse, and also in class.  If you attend such an event and write up a one-page, double-spaced summary of it, you will earn 2% on your semester grade.

You are allowed a maximum of 4% extra credit altogether.
