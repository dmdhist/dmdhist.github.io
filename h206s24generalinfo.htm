Course Description

The Middle Ages spanned more than a thousand years, from approximately 400 to 1500 AD, and included many different political, cultural, and ethnic communities of Europe and the Mediterranean region.  There was not one medieval civilization, but many medieval civilizations, related in some ways to each other, but distinct and constantly changing. This class will be an introduction to the history of the Middle Ages through its culture and ways of life.  

Because the period to be covered is so vast, we will focus our attention on seven communities that are representative of some aspects of medieval history.  We will look at villages and cathedral towns, manor houses and cities, and for each, we will consider who lived in the community, what activities and occupations they took pursued, what the community looked like physically, and what aspects of medieval life, politics, and culture are represented there.  In other words, we will get a sense of what the people in these spaces were doing and thinking.

Desired Learning Outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:
• knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, and ideas that shaped the cultures of Europe and the Mediterranean between 400 and 1500.
• facility in reading and understanding primary sources, including both texts and material culture
• the ability to deploy relevant evidence, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, including:
     - drawing inferences from primary sources and relating that information to broad course themes
     - quoting appropriately and contextually
     - using evidence from primary sources to answer particular questions about life and culture in the past

Structure

The class will consist primarily of lectures, but I encourage, and even require (see below) you to ask questions.   If you find something confusing, probably many other people do too, and they will appreciate your question also.

If at any time outside of class you have questions or wish to talk to me about any aspect of the class, or about history, or about anything else, please come to my office hours, as listed above.    If these times are not convenient, I will be happy to schedule an appointment; I can meet in-person or on zoom.   I am also very accessible via e-mail.

Course requirements

Attendance will be taken during each class; your percentage of classes attended will be worth 5% of your final grade.  You are allowed two 'free' absences - no excuse needed, and will not count against your attendance percentage.  If you are sick or have a valid emergency, email to let Prof. Deliyannis know and your absence will be excused (i.e. not count as one of your two 'free' absences).

In the third week of the semester, each of you will be assigned to a group made up of five or six people.  Each group will be assigned one class day. On your assigned day, you must sit in the front row of the classroom, and at some point during the lecture you must ask a question.   The question can be one of clarification, a request for further information, a question about something you read and didn't understand, or anything that relates in some way to the lecture.  If you ask your question, you will receive an A for this component of the course; if you do not ask a question, you will earn an F.   If you have an excused absence on your assigned day, email Prof. Deliyannis and she will reschedule you (forgetting that it was your day is not an excuse).  Please feel free to ask questions when it is not your turn, but this way everyone is guaranteed the opportunity to ask at least one question.

There are seven short (750-1000 words) papers assigned for this class; you must write four of them. THE FIRST PAPER, ON RAVENNA, IS REQUIRED. Then, of the remaining six papers, you must write three more of them. You may choose the topics that most interest you, or the ones that fit best with your schedule. All of the papers have two possible topics, you may write whichever you like.

For each paper you have a choice of two essay questions .  Guidelines for the papers can be found at this link; these are quite complicated and require you to do many things, so please read them carefully.   Papers are due at 11:30 am on the day they are due.  You must upload an electronic version (.doc, .docx, or .rtf; no .pdf) of your paper to the correct entry on Canvas Assignments.  If you send it late, you must email it to Prof. Deliyannis, who will upload it for you.  Late papers will be marked down one-half letter grade for every 24-hour period that they are late (this includes weekend days); after eight days, they will not be accepted.

You never know what crisis will befall you during the semester; it is strongly suggested that you turn in the first four papers rather than waiting until the end of the semester.  I cannot tell you how many students have waited until the end of the semester to write papers, only to discover that they had more to do at the end of the semester than they had realized.

Midterm and final essay exams will be given in-person, per the syllabus schedule.   A study guide will be given out two weeks before the midterm exam.  THE EXAMS WILL COVER MATERIAL FROM ALL UNITS, EVEN THE ONES FOR WHICH YOU DO NOT WRITE THE PAPERS.

Extra Credit

If you choose to write more than four papers, we will either drop the lowest paper grade(s) or the midterm exam grade, or you will be given extra credit of up to 2% on your semester average for the extra paper.  You will receive 1% extra for a B or B+ paper, and 2% for an A- or A paper. We will calculate your grade to your best advantage.

There will be other opportunities to earn extra credit during the semester, by attending events held on campus. These will be announced via Announcements on Canvas, and also in class.  If you attend such an event and write up a one-page, double-spaced summary of it, you will earn 2% on your semester grade.

You are allowed a maximum of 4% extra credit altogether.

Academic Integrity and Inclusion

As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code).  Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution.  Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty.  All suspected violations of the Code will be reported to the Office of Student Conduct and handled according to University policies.  Sanctions for academic misconduct may include a failing grade on the assignment, reduction in your final course grade, and a failing grade in the course, among other possibilities.  If you are unsure about the expectations for completing an assignment, be sure to seek clarification from Prof. Deliyannis in advance.

When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced.  Ignorance of what constitutes academic misconduct or plagiarism is not a valid excuse.  Any ideas or materials taken from another source for either written or oral use must be fully acknowledged in footnotes or endnotes.  Plagiarism (https://wts.indiana.edu/writing-guides/plagiarism.html) includes copying information from a paper written by someone else, from published material, or from a website, without citing your source.  The use of generative AI platforms for drafting or writing your papers will be considered plagiarism and/or cheating and will be reported to the Dean of Students (Office of Student Conduct) and handled according to University policies.
