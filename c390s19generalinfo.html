Description

The Roman empire has enjoyed mixed reviews since it ceased to exist. In modern popular culture, the Roman empire is usually depicted as an authoritarian, repressive, decadent, violent, and basically uptight institution; Edward Gibbon in the eighteenth century thought it was, at its height, a humane and enlightened political entity. The truth, of course, is somewhere in between. Nevertheless, everyone recognizes that it covered a large and very diverse territory, it lasted for a long time, and then it "fell".


The phrase "the decline and fall of the Roman empire", coined by Gibbon, evokes images of decadence, violence, and abrupt political and social upheaval. Ever since the "fall", people have been trying to make sense of why it fell, and whether its fall was a good or a bad thing; there are four shelves in the library with books on the "fall of the Roman empire". Some modern scholars, however, prefer to speak of "the transformation of the Roman world", emphasizing that the changes that took place in the late Roman empire were not necessarily symptomatic of 'decline', but rather of profound religious, social, and political transformations.


This course, however, will focus on questions of what "decline" and "fall" mean, and how we can evaluate these concepts. We will examine the Roman Empire, its neighbors, and its successors from the third through the sixth centuries A.D., and we will consider the political, cultural, intellectual, and religious factors that made the Roman empire what it was, and what happened to them in the wake of events such as the "barbarian invasions". Particular attention will be paid to what the people of the time thought was happening to the empire in which they lived, as seen through their writings.



Learning outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, religions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of western Europe from the third to the sixth centuries AD, related to the phenomenon known as the "fall of Rome.".

• understanding that the interpretation of a political/cultural/religous changes is dependent on the aims and interests of the interpreter

• facility in reading and understanding written primary sources in a variety of genres

• the ability to deploy relevant evidence from primary and secondary sources, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, to construct historical arguments about life and culture in the past

Requirements

The following are the requirements for this course:


Attendance                                                    8%

Five 4-6-page papers (12% each)                  60%

Final exam                                                    32%


Attendance will be taken in each class. You will be allowed two 'free' absences. If you are sick or have a valid emergency, let me know (email is fine) and your absence will be excused (i.e. will not count as one of your two 'free' absences).


Descriptions of the papers may be found at the end of this syllabus. Each paper is based on all of the readings, both primary and secondary, for the given unit. Because we will discuss the papers in class, they are due at the beginning of class on the day that they are due. Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (with no deadline other than the last day of classes). You do not have to use that option; remember that if a paper is late, it is not guaranteed to be graded before the next one is due.


The final exam will be an open-note test, and in particular will rely on the papers you have already written. You may use any notes, but you may NOT use books or photocopies. It behooves you, therefore, to attend class and take good notes; also to take good notes on readings and other materials.


There will probably be opportunities for extra credit during the semester; usually this involves attending a lecture and writing a one-page summary of it. These will be announced as the dates are publicized. Note that attending a lecture or session (and writing a summary) will earn you 2% on your semester average; I will give each person a maximum of 4% extra credit earned in this fashion. Summaries must be turned in one week after the event.



Academic Integrity

As a student at IU, you are expected to adhere to the standards detailed in the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct (Code). Academic misconduct (see here) is defined as any activity that tends to undermine the academic integrity of the institution. Violations include: cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, interference, violation of course rules, and facilitating academic dishonesty. When you submit an assignment with your name on it, you are signifying that the work contained therein is yours, unless otherwise cited or referenced. Any ideas or materials taken from another source for either written or oral use must be fully acknowledged. All suspected violations of the Code will be reported to the Dean of Students and handled according to University policies. Sanctions for academic misconduct may include a failing grade on the assignment, reduction in your final course grade, and a failing grade in the course, among other possibilities. If you are unsure about the expectations for completing an assignment or taking a test or exam, be sure to seek clarification from your instructor in advance.
