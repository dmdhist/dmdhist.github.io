Civic Riots in Chartres

In the late twelfth and early thirteenth century, when the cathedral was being built, some of the counts had died on crusades, leaving their widows with depleted incomes.  The countesses fought to keep or increase their economic rights at Chartres, especially by claiming jurisdiction over the cathedral precincts.  They did this because the chapter and bishop often brought town tradesmen to become serfs of the church and live in the cathedral area, and these were then exempt from the count's taxes and jurisdiction.

Early in 1210, the provost of the countess imprisoned a clerk of the choir and a serf of an archdeacon.  The chapter's court sentenced the provost to pay a fine.  The townspeople, probably provoked by the countess' officials, rioted, breaking into the chapter precincts and destroying property.  The chapter retaliated by going on strike, cancelling all except for essential church services.  The surviving document, which tells about the conclusion of the riot, says that the chapter appealed to the king for judgement.  The king's judges determined that the provost and other officials were to pay a large fine.  However, this settlement did not end the enmity; there were more riots in 1215 and 1249.  In 1253, after yet another riot, the chapter and bishop, fearing for their lives, fled from Chartres and lived in exile for five years, returning in 1258 only after the king took twenty citizens hostage and made 200 members of the trades, the agents of the count, and the 'people' to swear to do no further harm to the chapter.
