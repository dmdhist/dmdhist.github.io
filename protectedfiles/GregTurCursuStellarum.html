Gregory of Tours, De cursu stellarum ratio (MGH Rer. Scr. Mer. 1.2, pp. 404-422)[1]

                                                                                                           

IN CHRISTI NOMINE INCIPIT DE CURSUM STELLARUM RATIO, QUALITER AD OFFICIUM IMPLENDUM DEBEAT OBSERVARI.

 

 

(1)  Plerique philosophorum, dum studiis litterarum vacant, quasi plus ceteris septem scripsere miracula, ex quibus mihi quaedam praemittere et alia plus admiranda libuit memorare, quorum haec habentur vel formae vel opera.

 

IN THE NAME OF CHRIST HERE BEGINS AN ACCOUNT OF THE COURSES OF THE STARS, HOW EACH MAY BE OBSERVED AD OFFICIUM IMPLENDUM

 

(1)  Many philosophers, when they had leisure for literary studies, wrote of seven marvels greater than others, of which I am pleased to set before you and to remember other things more admirable, of which these things have either the appearance or the workmanship.

 

(2)  PRIMUM ERGO miraculum ponimus Noe arcam, quae Domini ore, qualis fierit, est mandata; cuius lungitudo trecentorum, latitudo quinquagenta, altitudo tregenta cubitorum est habita.  Quam arcam bicamaratam et tricamaratam legimus, cuius omne opus in cubito consumatum est. Fenestram sive ostium a latere habuit.  In eam enim de omnibus volatilibus caeli ac besteis terrae sive reptilibus cum hominibus octo ad reparatione mundi per inluviem cataclysmi genera reservata sunt.

 

(2)  We place as the first marvel the ark of Noah, whose form was commanded by the mouth of the Lord; which had a length of 300 cubits, a width of 50 cubits, and a height of 30 cubits.  We read that this ark had two compartments and three compartments, of which the whole work was finished in one cubit.  It had a window or door on the side.  For in it were reserved examples from all the flying creatures of the heavens and beasts or reptiles of the earth, along with eight humans, for the restoration of the world after the flood.

 

(3)  SECUNDUM ponimus Babiloniam, cuius iuxta Horosium haec est expositio:[2] Mira campi planitie undique conspicua, laetissima castrorum faciem, moenibus paribus per quadrum disposita; murorum eius vix credibilis relatu firmitas et magnitudo, latitudine cubitorum 50, altitudine quater tantum.  Ceterum ambitus eius 470 stadiis circumvenitur; murus coctili e lapide adque infusu bitumine conpactus; a fronte murorum centum portae aereae.  Ipsa autem longitudo in consumatione pinnarum, utroque latere habitaculis defensorum aeque dispositis, media intercapidine vicinas quatrigas capit; domos intrinsecus quater geminae, habitationes minaci proceritate mirabiles.  Haec prima post humani generis reparatione a Nebroth gigante condita est.

 

(3)  Second we place Babylon, about which these things are told by Orosius:  on all sides marvellously conspicuous on a level plain, delightfully arranged like a camp, with surrounding walls formed into a square; the strength and size of its walls is hardly credible to relate, fifty cubits thick and four times as high.  Its circumference surrounded 470 stades; the wall, constructed of baked brick with bitumen for mortar; in the circuit of the walls 100 bronze gates.  The thickness of the walls accomodated small turrets for defenders placed evenly along either side of the wall, in the central space there was room for a four-horse chariot; the houses inside were of eight stories, marvellous for their menacing height.  This city was first founded after the restoration of the human race by the giant Nebrot.

 

(4)  TERTIUM est templum Salomonis, qui non tantum in magnitudine fabricae, quantum in ornamentis miraculum fuit.[3] 

 

Aedificavit parietis domus intrinsecus tabolatis cedrinis a pavimento domus usque ad summitatem parietum et usque ad laquearia, operuitque lignis intrinsecus et texuit pavimentum domus tabulis abiegnis.  Aedificavitque 20 cubitorum ad posteriorem partem templi tabolata cedrina a pavimento usque ad superiora et fecit interiorem domum oraculi in sancta sanctorum.  Porro 40 cubitorum erat ipsum templum pro foribus oraculi, et cedro omnis domus intrinsecus vestiebatur, habens tornaturas et iuncturas suas fabricatas et celaturas eminentes; omnia cedrinis tabulis vestiebantur, nec omnino lapis apparere poterat in pariete. 

 

Oraculum autem in medio domus interiore fecerat, ut poneret ibi arcam federis Domini.  Porro autem oraculum habebat 20 cubitos longitudinis et vigenti cubitos latitudinis, operuitque illud adque vestivit auro purissimo; sed et altare vestivit cedro.  Domum quoque ante altare operuit auro purissimo et adfixit laminas clavis aureis.  Nihilque erat in templum quod non auro tegeretur; et totum altare oraculi texit auro. 

 

Et fecit in oraculo duo Chaerubin de lignis olivarum decem cubitorum altitudinis; quinque cubitorum ala Chaerubin una, id est decem cubitos habentes a summitatem alae usque ad alae alterius summitate.  Decim quoque cubitorum erat Chaerubin secundus mensuram parem, posuitque Chaerubin in medio templi interioris texitque eos auro. 

 

Et omnes parietes per circuitum scalpsit et variis caelaturis et turno et fecit in eis Chaerubin et palmas et picturas varias quasi prominentes de pariete et egredientes, sed et pavimentum domus texit auro intrinsecus et extrinsecus. 

 

Et in ingressum oraculi fecit ostiola de lignis olivarum, postes quadrangulorum quinque et duo ostia de lignis olivarum, et sculpsit in eis pictura Chaerubin et palmarum species et anaclifa valde prominentia et texit ea auro.  Fecitque introitum templi postes de lignis olivarum quadrangulatos et duo ostia de lignis abiegnis altrensecus; utrumque ostium duplex erat et se invicem tenens operiebatur.  Et sculpsit Chaerubin et palmas et celaturas valde eminentes. 

 

Multa quidem et alia inibi fecit admirabilia, quae prosequi longum videtur.

 

(4)  The third is the Temple of Solomon, which was a marvel not so much because of the size of its fabric, but rather because of its decoration. 

 

He built the walls of the house on the inside with cedar panels from the pavement of the house up to the top of the walls and up to the roofbeams, and he covered the interior with wood and he constructed the floor of the house with planks of fir.  And he built up cedar planks 20 cubits at the back part of the temple from the floor up to the top, and he made the inner part of the house a sanctuary to be the Holy of Holies.  And this temple was 40 cubits before the doors of the sanctuary, and the whole house was clad inside with cedar, having its turnings and joints artfully fabricated and carvings projected out; all was clad in cedar planks, nor was any stone visible in the wall. 

 

However he made the sanctuary in the middle of inner part of the house, and he placed there the ark of the covenant of the Lord.  And the sanctuary was 20 cubits long and 20 cubits wide, and he covered it and overlaid it with purest gold; and he overlaid the altar with cedar.  Also the house in front of the altar he covered with purest gold and he attached the sheets with gold nails.  There was nothing in the temple that was not covered with gold; and he covered the whole altar of the sanctuary with gold. 

 

And he made in the sanctuary two Cherubim of olive branches ten cubits high; one wing of a Cherub was five cubits, that is having ten cubits from the tip of one wing to the tip of the other wing.  And the second Cherub was also ten cubits in the same measure, and he placed the Cherubim in the middle of the interior of the temple and covered them with gold. 

 

And he engraved all the walls around with various carvings and figures and he made in them Cherubim and palm-trees and various pictures as if standing out and coming forth from the wall, and the floor of the house he covered with gold inside and out.

 

And at the entrance of the sanctuary he made a little door of olive wood, five squared posts and two doors of olive-wood, and he carved on them pictures of Cherubim and palm-trees and carvings standing out and he covered them with gold.  And he made at the entrance of the temple squared posts of olive-wood and two doors of fir-wood outside; and each door was double and opened with folding leaves.  And he carved Cherubim and palm trees and carved work standing out very much.

 

Indeed he made there many other admirable things, which it seems too long to recount.

(5) QUARTUM est sepulchrum regis Persici ex uno lapide amitisto cavato miroque opere sculptum ac interrasile et extrinsecus habens effigies hominum, bistiarum seu avium in foris prominentes; arbores quoque sculptas habet cum foliis et pomis opere celato.

 

(6) QUINTUM est statua colossi Rodo insolae collocata ex aere fusile, cuius tam inmensa est altitudo, ut vix lapidem capiti eius aliquis possit inicere, et deaurata est.  Ferunt quoque multi, per tibiam eius usque ad capud hominem posse ascendere, si aditum unde ingrederetur haberet; adserunt etiam, capud huius statuae recipere posse tritici choros duos et viginti.

 

(7) SEXTUM est theatrum quod in Eraclea habetur ex uno monte factum, ita ut omne ex uno latere sit expletum, tam extrinsecus parietes quam intrinsecus arcus, foveae, gradus, sedilia; et omne opus eius ex lapide uno conpletum est; est autem marmore Heracleo vestitum.

 

(8) SEPTIMUM est pharus Alexandrina, quae super quattuor mirae magnitudinis cancros constructa habetur; nec enim hi parvi esse poterant, qui tam inmensum sustinent vel altitudinis vel latitudinis pondus.  Nam ferunt, super unum quemque brachium cancri si homo extensus iaceat, eum operire non possit.  Pharus autem iste, datis de publico paleis, nocte succenditur, scilicet ut nocturno tempore errantes nautae vento vel imbre, si stellas videre non potuerint, sciant, qua parte dirigant vela.

 

(5)  The fourth marvel is the tomb of the Persian king, carved from one amethyst stone and sculpted with marvellous workmanship and inscribed, and having on the outside projecting images of men, animals or birds on the doors; it also had sculpted trees with leaves and fruit, of engraved work.

 

(6)  The fifth is the statue of the Colossus of Rhodes, erected of molten bronze on an island, whose height is so immense that one can scarcely cast a stone up to its head, and it is gilded.  Also many are able to climb through the leg up to the man's head, if it had an entrance whence one might enter; for they claim that the head of this statue could receive 22 choros of wheat.

 

 

(7)  The sixth is the the theater at Heraclea, which is made from one mountain, so that everything might be completed on one side, both the walls on the exterior and the arches, pits, stairs, and seats on the inside; and all its work is made of one stone; it is covered with Heraclean marble.

 

(8)  The seventh is the Lighthouse of Alexandria, which is said to be built on four vaults of marvellous size; for nor could they be small, which hold up such an immense weight of height and breadth.  For they bear, if a man should lie flat on one arm of the vault he could not cover it.  This lighthouse, with bundles of wood given by the public, at night is set on fire, so that during the night ships wandering by wind or rain, if they cannot see the stars, they know where they should direct their sails.

(9) Sed ista, licet quaepiam iussionem Dei, quaepiam autem adinventionem humana constructa sint, ab hominibus tamen constat esse fundata, ideoque et quaedam deruerunt, quaedam autem ruinae sunt proxima. 

 

Nam sunt alia quae ipse omnipotens Deus noster proprio opere in hoc mundo vel per dies singulos renovat, vel post transacto anni curriculum repraesentat, quae et in locupletatione ostenduntur muneris, ut est commotio oceani et fructus terrae; alia vero in ostensione proferuntur virtutis, ut est sol, luna, stellae, Phinix; et quaedam ex his peccatores arguunt et ignem infernalem figurant, ut est Ethna, fons Gratianopolitanus.  Haec sunt enim miracula, quae nulla aetate seniscunt, nullo occasu occidunt, nulla labe minuuntur, nisi cum Dominus mundum dissolvi praeciperit.

 

(9)  But it is the case that these things were established by men, some indeed at the order of God, but some were built from human invention, and therefore some have fallen, and some are close to ruin. 

 

But there are other things which our almighty God by his own labor either renews in this world every day, or displays in the course of the year, and some are shown as enriching gifts, such as the movement of the ocean and the fruits of the earth; others indeed are produced as demonstration of his excellence, like the sun, moon, stars, and Phoenix; and some of them declare to sinners and represent the fire of hell, like Etna, and the springs of Grenoble.  For these are Wonders which will not grow old with age, will not fall by accident, will not be diminished by ruin, except when the Lord commands the world to dissolve.

(10) PRIMUM est ergo omnium maris oceani commutio, in qua ita per dies singulos dilatatur, ut accedens ora litoris repleat rursumque recedens siccum praebeat iter; tunc populis per humum aridam gradientibus piscium sive diversorum liguminum multitudo copiosa collegitur.  Hoc primum miraculum Deus humano generi praeparavit, quod et admiratione praeclarum sit et congruum servituti.

 

(10)  The first of all of these is the motion of the seas of the ocean, which on each day is enlarged, so that advancing it fills the coastal shores and receding again reveals a dry path; then a plentiful multitude of fish or various seaweeds are collected by people walking on the dry ground.  God prepared this first miracle for the human race, that it might be notable in admiration and suitable for servitude(?).

(11) SECUNDUM est simile huic, de granis scilicit frugum et de fructibus arborum, cum iacta terrae simina et sulcis operta, adveniente aestate eriguntur in culmina, ornataque comis et spicis lacteo intrinsecus adipe saginantur, quae Dominus spiritalis doctrinae sator de profecto verborum suorum, quae in populos iaciebat, parabolice commutavit, dicens: Sic est verbum Dei, quemadmodum si iactet homo semen, et exurgat nocte et diae; ultro enim terra fructificat, primum herbam, deinde spicam, deinde plenum granum in spicam.[4]  Paulus autem apostolus de resurrectione hanc rationem figuravit, dicens: Quod seminas non vivificatur, nisi prius moriatur.  Sic est, inquit, et resurrectio mortuorum: seminatur in infirmitate, surgit in virtute,[5] et cetera. 

 

Aequa est enim et arborum natura, quae, ut puto, ipsam resurrectionem signat, cum in hieme nudatae foliis tamquam mortui habentur, verno vero tempore ornantur foliis, decorantur floribus, pomisque aestate replentur.  Quod miraculum quamquam hac utatur similitudinem, ad praesens tamen beneficium populis praestat, ut cognoscat homo, ab illo se accipere victum, qui eum creavit ex nihilo.

 

(11)  The second is similar to this, namely from the seeds of grain and the fruits of trees, when the seeds cast on the ground and concealed in furrows, with the arrival of summer they grow to a height, and they are ornamented with leaves and ears and their insides are crammed with milky fat, which the Lord, sower of spiritual doctrine metaphorically transformed, for the effect of his words, which he cast out to the people, saying, "Thus is the word of the Lord, as if a man should scatter seed, and [should sleep] and rise by night and day; for the earth produces of itself, first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear."  But the apostle Paul described this reason for the resurrection, saying, "What you sow does not live, unless first it dies.  So it is," he says, "with the resurrection of the dead:  it is sown in weakness, it is raised in power," etc. 

 

Equal to this is the nature of trees, which, as I think, signify the resurrection, since in the winter, bare of leaves, they are like the dead, but in the springtime they are bedecked with leaves, are decorated with flowers, and in the summer they are full of fruit.  Although this marvel uses a metaphor, it demonstrates at the present a benefit to the people, so that man may recognize that he will be conquered by him who created him from nothing.

(12) TERTIUM est, quod de Phinice Lactantius refert.[6]  Magna est, ait, corpore et decora plumis unguibusque et oculis, quae alterius avis non est iuncta consortio nec iuncta coniugio; nam et nescire homines manifestum est, cuius sit generis, masculus an femina sive neutrum.  Haec postquam mille transierit annos, petit locum omnibus locis mundanis celsitudinem praeminentem, in quo habetur locus viridi comam verno hibernoque perdurans, in cuius medium fons est magnus et ubertate profluus et lenitate praeclarus. 

 

Huius in litore arbor nobilis reliquas luci arbores proceritate praecellens; in huius arboris vertice haec avis de diversis pigmentorum generibus construit sibi seu nidum sive sepulchrum, insedensque in medio eius ore odoramenta adtrahit seque ex his tegit.  Tunc diversis modolis incipit cantos effundere suaves, ac de nido exiliens, aquarum se undis inmergit.  Quod cum ter quaterque repetierit, ascendit iterum super nidum adtrahitque denuo super se odoramenta, quae detulit.  Imicante autem sole primum ei iubar inferit ignem, adprehensumque nidum, tota integre concrematur. 

 

Tunc pulves exustus ad se collegitur et in unam massam quasi in ovi speciae conglobatur; hac exinde iterum surgens, resumta vita, nutritur, nec cuiquam homini, dum inplumis est, pascere cura est.  Tantum caelesti rore nutrita, ad pristinam speciem revocatur et ipsius figurae eisdem plumis eidemque colore reparata progreditur, ut prius fuerat ante morte.  Quod miraculum resurrectionem humanam valde figurat et ostendit, qualiter homo luteus redactus in pulvere, sit iterum de ipsis favillis tuba canente resuscitandus.

 

(12)  The third is what Lactantius reports about the Phoenix.  He says it is of large size and endowed with feathers and claws and eyes, which bird is united neither with a consort nor a spouse; for it is not manifest for men to know what sex it is, masculine or feminine or neuter.  This bird, after 1000 years have passed, seeks a place notable in altitude from all other earthly places, in which there is a place in which green foliage endures through spring and winter, in the middle of which is a great spring both abundant in flow and brilliant in gentleness. 

 

On the edge of this is a noble tree, superior in height to other trees in the grove; at the tip of this tree this bird constructs for itself a nest (or a tomb) of different types of colors, and sitting in the middle of it it collects sweet-smelling substances and using her mouth covers itself with these.  Then in various modes it begins to pour out sweet songs, and leaving the nest, it submerges itself in the waves of water.  When it has repeated this three or four times, it goes back up to the nest and finally collects on top of itself the sweet-smelling substances, which it has brought.  With the sun sparkling, however, first its radiance produces fire, and consuming the nest, the whole is completely burned. 

 

Then the ashy dust is collected together and in one mass made into a ball in the shape of an egg; rising back again from this, with life resumed, it feeds, nor by any man, while it is without feathers, is there a care to feed it.  Nourished by heavenly dew, it returns to its former shape and its figure progresses, repaired to the same feathers and same color, just as it had been before death.  This marvel clearly prefigures the human resurrection and shows how man made of mud, reduced to dust, may again be resurrected from those embers by the singing trumpet.

(13) QUARTUM est Ethna mons Siciliae insulae datus, qui vivis ardoribus exaestuat, flammasque evomens validas, ac terribiliter regione sulphor eructuat; de quo Publeus Mantuanus in tertio Ineidum libro ita ait:[7]

Portus ab accessu ventorum inmotus et ingens

Ipse, sed horrefecis iuxta tonat Ethna ruinis,

Interdumque atra prorumpit ad aethera nubem

Turbine fumantem piceo et candente favilla,

Adtollitque globos flammarum et sidera lambit.

Sed et si qua in foraminibus, unde haec egrediuntur flammae, iniecta fuerint, mox evomuntur.  Meminet et huius montis et ille Iulius Titianus his verbis, dicens: Montes maximi in Sicilia quattuor, Ericus, Nebrodes, Neptunius et Ethna, quem videns saepius flammas e vertice volvere, idque sentire orbis prope fide credentium, quamquam id: cum primum Romae nuntiatum est, arsisse Ethnam, in monstris procuratum est.[8]

 

(13)  The fourth is Mount Etna, on the island of Sicily, which burns with living blazes, vomiting great flames, and belches out sulphur terribly in that region; of which Publius the Mantuan, in book three of the Aeneid, thus said:

There lies a harbour, safe from the winds’ approach and spacious in itself, but near at hand Aetna thunders with terrifying crashes, and now hurls forth to the sky a black cloud, smoking with pitch-black eddy and glowing ashes, and uplifts balls of flame and licks the stars.

But anything which is cast into the cracks, whence these flames exit, is soon spat out again.  And Julius Titianus also remembers this mountain with these words, saying, "The four greatest mountains in Sicily, Ericus, Nebrodes, Neptunius and Etna, seeing which most often erupting flames from its summit, and to sense of the globe almost with faith of believers, although this:  when first it was told at Rome that Etna was burning, it was interpreted as a divine omen."

(14) QUINTUM est de fontibus Gratianopolitanis, de quibus simul et latix manat et ignis.  Nam videas limphis flammas supervolare, hauris aquam de medio foci nec uriris, bibis nec incenderis, portas nec ab igne conprehenderis.  Quod si cereum vel tedas admoveas, protinus ut flammas adtigerint, conprehenduntur; manum quoque si inicias, non crematur.  Hinc Elarius quidam ait:[9]

Si vere exurunt ignes, cur vivitis undae?

Si vere extingunt undae, cur vivitis ignis?

Limpharum in gremiis inimicus condidit ignis,

Communesque ortus imperat alta manus, et reliqua.

O admirabile potentiae divinae misterium! Una vena fontis et ignis producit et latices, scilicet ut cognoscant omnes dominationi eius et refrigerium gloriosae vitae et iudicium aeternae mortis adsistere, intellegantque, incendiis licentiam esse non datam ad nocendum corpus humanum, quod post iudicium, si peccato obnoxium fuerit, accepturi erunt perpetualiter exurendum.

 

(14)  The fifth is the springs of Grenoble, in which simultaneously there remains liquid and fire.  For you may see flames fly over the waters, you may draw out water from the middle of the fire nor do you burn, you drink but you do not burn, you carry but you are not consumed by the fire.  Because if you throw in a grain or pitch, immediately as the flames touch it, it is consumed; also if you put in your hand, it does not burn.  Hence a certain Hilarius said:

If truly fires burn out, why do you waves survive?

If truly waves extinguish, why do you fires survive?

In the bosom of water fire is the enemy,

And the high hand rules the common origin, and the opposite.

O admirable mystery of divine power!  A single vein of the spring produces produces both fire and liquid, namely so that all might recognize the His power and existence of the refreshment of glorious life and the judgement of eternal death, and might understand that by fire, license is not given to kill the human body, but that after judgement, if one has been guilty of sin, they will be perpetually consumed by fire.

(15) SEXTUM est illud, quod licit ante ista Dei opere fuerit conditum, tamen pro ratione huius rei locatur extremum, hoc est, quod de solis officio creatur omnium Deus praecepit caelebrari; nam non sine grande admiratione cernitur, quod ipse sol omnibus diebus quasi famulans mundo ingerit lumen, quod orientem occidentemque perlustrat, quod calore suo terras fecundans, frugem gignere facit.

 

(15)  The sixth is something which was indeed established by the work of God before these other things, however by reason of this thing is located last, that is about the office of the sun, that God creator of all commanded to be celebrated; for it appears not without great admiration, that the sun itself, like a servant, every day brings light to the world that it illuminates east and west, that making fertile the lands by its heat, it makes grain grow.

(16) SEPTIMUM est enim miraculum, qualiter luna in ter quinis diebus vel crescit ad integritatem vel ad exiguitate minuitur.  Admiramur et illud, quod stellae oriente ortae decedunt occidenti, et quaedam ex his in medio caeli apparentes, quaedam aquilone propinquantes, in circuitu rotentur nec rectam faciant viam, et quaepiam toto videantur anno, quaepiam menses in quibus appareant habeant definitos. 

 

De quo cursu, si Deus iubeat, velim, de quanto experimentum accipi, rationem nescientibus dare.  Sed nomina quae his vel Maro vel reliqui indiderunt poetae postpono, tantum ea vocabula nuncupans, quae vel usitate rusticitas nostra vocat vel ipsorum signaculorum expremit ordo, ut est crux, falcis vel reliqua signa; quia non ego in his mathesim doceo neque futura perscrutare praemoneo, sed, qualiter cursus in Dei laudibus rationabiliter impleatur, exhortor, vel quibus horis qui in hoc officio adtente versari cupit, debeat nocte consurgere vel Dominum deprecare.

(16)  And the seventh marvel is how the moon in fifteen days either increases to full or diminshes to thinness.  And we admire this, that the stars that rise in the east set in the west, and some of them appear in the middle of the sky, some neighboring the north, they are rotated in a circuit, nor do they make a straight path, and while some are seen throughout the year, some have defined months in which they appear. 

 

Of this course, if God permits, I wish, insofar I have undertaken the experiment, to give the ignorant an account.  But I will disregard the names which either Maro or the other poets have given, rather recounting those words which either our own rusticity customarily calls them or the order of those signs expresses, that is the cross, the sickle, or other constellations; since in these things I do not teach the mathematics nor forewarn to examine the future, but, I exhort in what way the cycle of praises of God might rationally be fulfilled, or for him who desires to consider heedfully at what hours in this office he might be able to wake up at night or praise the Lord.
