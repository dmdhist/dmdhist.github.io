Commerce and Trade in Cairo
(AD 970-1200)


Located at the beginning of the Nile Delta, and having easy access to various Mediterranean ports, Fustat became a center of trade soon after its foundation. The Fatimids established al-Qahira as their center of government, but Fustat remained the place where trade and manufacturing were carried out. In the Fatimid period and later, a large number of different types of industries can be identified in Fustat - indeed one scholar has identified 598 different manufacturing occupations from documents including those of the genizah.

Around 20% of the labor force was related to the textile industry: spinners, weavers, fullers, carders, dyers (of cotton, wool, silk, and linen), cloth beaters, button makers, lace makers, makers of turbans, makers of skirts, furriers, embroiderers, makers of rope, tassels, ribbons, tents, carpets, curtains, cushions, and bedding. Another 20% was involved in food production - making bread, wine, cheese, sugar, etc. 8-16% of the labor force was involved in metal- or leather-working, and another 8-16% worked in construction. Carpenters, potters, paper-makers, ivory-carvers, basket-makers, and processors of chemical substances each constitute 1-6%. And finally, smaller numbers produced essential oils, varnishes, paints, medicinal drugs, ink, lime, ointments, and perfumes.

Most of these industries were organized as individual artisan shops, but there were also state-organized textile workshops, some of which produced luxury items specifically for government gifts. There is no indication of social or legal organization by type of craft; instead, organization and transmission of skills seems to have been done privately, often from father to son. The textile industry, and especially embroiders, dyers, and spinners, included women as workers who earned wages, often working from their homes.

Some industries were dominated by particular religious or ethnic groups. Christians dominate ivory-carving, stucco- and mosaic-making; Jews controlled silk dyeing and the manufacture of metal and glass items. Rock crystal-carving was carried out by Persians. Other industries were open to all, and the inhabitants of Fustat worked together to create a vibrant economy.

In addition to items grown or manufactured in the region, Fustat became a center of an international commerce that spanned the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean. New crops were imported into Egypt; those that grew well and became widely popular included sugar cane, rice, eggplant, Swiss chard, oranges, bananas, and watermelon.  Items that could be traded over long distances included spices from India, cheese from Sicily, pistachios and olives from Syria, honey from Greece, Catalonia, and Provence, olive oil from Spain, France, and Tunisia, saffron from Italy and Spain, and dried fruits and nuts from everywhere.

The ceramic industry of Fustat produced a wide range of vessels, including oil lamps, for all classes of people. Here is a selection of pottery fragments from the Fatimid period, showing what would have been available to a Cairo household.

One of the types of pottery for which Fustat was particularly well-known in the Fatimid period was lusterware. Using a glaze made from a copper and silver compound gives the decoration a metallic shimmer, and would have been a deluxe style. The technology may have been brought from Iraq, but Fatimid lusterware from Egypt is found throughout the Mediterranean.

An art/craft form particular to Fatimid Egypt was the water jug filter. As part of the manufacture of water-jugs, a filter would be built into the neck of the jar, presumably either to strain the water as it was gathered, or to protect the water in the jug from insects and debris. Thousands of these have been excavated, and they range from simple geometric patterns to beautifully intricate designs around pictures of animals or men. They were found in all sorts of contexts, from poor to wealthy, so this is an example of an art form that many inhabitants of the city would have experienced directly.
