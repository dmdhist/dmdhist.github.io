The Roman Empire engulfed a wide array of people who were subject to the constant change and fluctuation that occurred during the period.  Despite this constant change the empire has been recognized for its success and longevity.  Central to Roman life were cities.  These acted as centers of trade, government, and culture (Lecture, Jan. 15).  They were also similar in that they were symbolic places used for the spread of Roman Culture and values (Lecture, Jan. 15). Under these similarities Roman cities and the empire in general "lay mind-boggling diversity---a babble of languages, a throng of many races, and a great diversity of religion (Textbook, 8).  Despite this around 313 the acceptance and eventual spread of Christianity seemed to unite the empire (Textbook 22).  The spread of Christianity, facilitated great change in the Roman Empire by providing leadership that challenged the emperor, affecting all aspects of life in a late Antique city such as Ravenna.

            Cities such as Ravenna were profoundly affected by the spread of Christianity.  This was most evident in the leadership of the city and even the empire.  Leadership was arranged in a strict hierarchical arrangement (Notitia Dignitatum).  Before Christianity smaller cities like Ravenna were often governed by well-off citizens who acted as agents of Rome.  The administrators served Rome by "administering its edicts, emulating its traditions, and of course, collecting its taxes (Textbook, 12).  With the acceptance of Chritianity authority figures within the church quickly assumed authority positions in the empire and cities.  This can be viewed in 325 at the council of Nicaea when Constantine called together the leaders of the church to discuss the possible views of the Church (Lecture, Jan. 20).

            The power of the emperor being challenged by the new church was most evident in the year 374 in the city of Milan.  It was here that the city's bishop St. Ambrose challenged the emperor's power.  The emperor ordered that the church go from being Orthodox to Arian (Epistle XX).  In an answer addressing if all thing belong to the Emperor, Ambrose Responds "Do not, O Emperor, lay on yourself the burden of such a thought on that you have any imperial power over those things which belong to God (Epistle XX)."  This demonstrated that in cities across the Rome the church had become a rival to power in the empire.  Ambrose said "the palaces belong to the Emperor, the churches to the Bishop (Epistle XX)."  The power of the church grew during the late stages of the empire when Bishops became increasing involved in imperial government, often checking on the activities of Roman officials and even sometimes assuming political control over there dioceses (Textbook, 35)

            Like the bishop in Milan, the bishop in Ravenna had to have also assumed a leadership role.  This would have been aided after the year of 400 when Ravenna becomes a capital (Lecture, Jan. 20).  In doing so the city was greatly enlarged.  The orthodox cathedral, bell tower, and baptistery were also built during this period (Lecture, Jan 20).  Unlike Milan however Ravenna did not always remain completely orthodox.  When the Germanic tribes the Ostrogoths conquered Italy in 489 their king Theodoric allowed the Orthodox Christians to keep their church but built a separate cathedral in Ravenna for the Arians (Ravenna, 2004).  As Christianity grew so did the influence of the church on the Roman Empire's politics and even the buildings within their cities. 

            Besides changing the leadership structure of the Roman Empire, Christianity also facilitated the creation of some of the finest buildings and paintings of the Roman Empire.  For the wealthy of Rome, especially the emperors, building a church was a pious act (Lecture, Jan.22).  It was believed the greater the church the greater the emperor who built it.  After taking over Ravenna the Germanic king Theodoric (493-526), in a letter to Sun, Vir Illustics and Comes says "let nothing lie useless which may rebound to the beauty of the city (Letters of Theodoric)."  This shows that he recognized that the beauty and upkeep of his empire reflected upon his own leadership.  It also began to demonstrate how Rome's conquerors through Christianity identified with the Roman way of life.   In the case of Theodoric after his successful conquest he hoped to build upon the success of Rome.  In a letter to Unigis he says "we delight to live after the law of the Romans, who we seek to defend with our arms (Letters of Theodoric)."  Theodoric had spent much of his youth in Constantinople and may have been attempting to preserve the culture that he knew. (Letters of Theodoric)

            Despite growing up in Constantinople Theodoric did not adopt all of Rome's beliefs and ideals.  Rather than accepting orthodox Christianity he like most Germanic people chose to follow Arian Christianity.   This demonstrated that despite Christianity's unifying effect on Rome this issue of Orthodox Christianity vs. Arian Christianity produced deep divides.  It lead to the Eastern Emperor Valens (364-378), an Arian Christian, to permit the Visigoths (also Arians), to cross peacefully into the empire (Textbook, 41).  This would eventually lead to the Visigoths capturing Rome in 410 (Textbook, 41).  This division within the church also contributed to the building and taking over of churches.  To accommodate Arians Theodoric chose to build the church of the Holy Spirit (Ravenna, 2004).  Justinian (527-565), on the other had after retaking Ravenna around 554 chose to convert the existing Arian Church as well as the ones being constructed into Arian Churches.  Justinian changed Theodora's Arian Church St. Martin's into the Orthodox St. Apollinare Nuovo (Lecture Jan. 27).  He even went as far as to have many of the mosaics changed.  Justinian also had the church San Vitale designed in such a way to portray the presence of the emperor (Lecture, Jan. 27).

            Change in the Roman Empire was a gradual process that was greatly affected by the acceptance of Christianity.  Christianity established new leaders who would change the emperor's claim of complete power.  It would also facilitate the growth of both buildings and art work.  These changes can be seen in a late antique city like Ravenna.  Ravenna seemed to be constantly acquiring new leadership with new ideals in Christianity which would in turn affect the institutions, building, and population groups within the city.
