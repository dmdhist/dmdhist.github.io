Jean Froissart, Chronicles, on the Peasants' Revolt of 1381

 

Jean Froissart (c1337-c1410) was a poet, scholar and author who wrote a chronicle of the Hundred Years War. He was born in France to a family of businessmen and moneylenders. His gift for poetry brought him to the attention of the French nobility. In 1361, he became the court poet to the household of Queen Philippa, wife of England's King Edward III.  When she died in 1369, he was already in Brussels, and he remained in the Netherlands for the rest of his life. That year, he began to write his Chronicles.  Froissart relied on eyewitness accounts for much of his information, but he also tried to provide some analysis of the "big picture" surrounding events. His love of the nobility, and his tendency to embellish his writing in the interest of providing a good story, have led historians to question the accuracy of his work.

 

As Froissart tells, in 1381 a group of peasants rose in revolt against a tax that they felt was being levied unfairly; King Richard II, who was only 14 years old, was felt to be being influenced by an unpopular group of nobles.  The rebels, inflamed by Wat Tyler and John Ball, marched down toward London, demanding the dismissal of some of the unpopular advisors and an end to serfdom.  The stormed the Tower of London and executed various people there, including the Archbishop of Canterbury.  The next day, King Richard gave in to demands that he meet personally with the rebels at Smithfield.  Tyler bahaved rudely to the king, and the mayor of London killed him; the rebel army was tricked into moving, and was then defeated by the royal forces.

 

 

How The Commons Of England Rebelled Against The Noblemen

 

In the meantime . . . there befell in England great mischief and rebellion of movement of the common people, by which deed England was at a point to have been lost without recovery. There was never realm nor country in so great adventure as it was in that time, and all because of the ease and riches that the common people had, which moved them to this rebellion, as earlier they had done in France, which did much hurt, for by such incidents the realm of France has been greatly troubled.

 

It was a marvellous thing and a disgrace that this mischief began in England, and to give example to all manner of people I will speak of it as it was done, as I was informed, and of the incidents thereof.

 

There was an usage in England, and yet is in various counties, that the noblemen have great franchise over the commons and keep them in forced labor, that is to say, according to custom their tenants must labor on the lords' lands, gather and bring home their grain, and thresh and fan it, and by forced labor make their hay and hew their wood and bring it home. All these things they must do by forced labor, and there are more of these people in England than in any other kingdom. Thus the noblemen and prelates are served by them, and especially in the counties of Kent, Essex, Sussex and Bedford.

 

These unhappy people of these said counties began to stir, because they said they were kept in great forced labor, and in the beginning of the world, they said, there were no bondmen, wherefore they maintained that none ought to be bound, unless he did treason to his lord, as Lucifer did to God; but they said they could have no such battle, for they were neither angels nor spirits, but men formed in the same way as their lords, saying why should they then be kept so under like beasts; which they said they would no longer suffer, for they would be all one, and if they labored or did anything for their lords, they would have wages for it as well as other things.

 

And of this opinion was a foolish priest in the county of Kent called John Ball, for which foolish words he had been three times in the bishop of Canterbury's prison: for this priest used oftentimes on Sundays after mass, when the people were going out of the church, to go into the cloister and preach, and made the people to assemble about him, and would say thus

 

"Ah, ye good people, things are not going well in England, nor will they until everything is owned in common, and there are no villeins nor gentlemen, but we are all united together, and the lords be no greater masters than we are.  What have we deserved, or why should we be kept thus in forced labor? We are all come from one father and one mother, Adam and Eve: whereby can they say or show that they are greater lords than we are, except that they cause us to earn and labor for that which they spend?  They are clothed in velvet and camlet furred with grise, and we are covered with poor cloth: they have their wines, spices and good bread, and we have the drawing out of the chaff and drink water: they dwell in fair houses, and we have the pain and travail, rain and wind in the fields; and by the product of our labors they keep and maintain their estates: we are called their bondmen, and unless we readily do them service, we are beaten; and we have no sovereign to whom we may complain, nor that will hear us nor do us right. Let us go to the king, he is young, and show him what forced labor we are in, and show him how we will have it otherwise, or else we will provide ourselves some remedy; and if we go together, all manner of people that are now in any bondage will follow us to the intent to be made free; and when the king sees us, we shall have some remedy, either by fairness or otherwise."

 

Thus John Ball said on Sundays, when the people issued out of the churches in the villages; wherefore many of the lower-class people loved him, and those that intended to no goodness said how he spoke the truth; and so they would murmur to one another in the fields and in the roads as they went together, affirming how John Ball spoke the truth.

 

The archbishop of Canterbury, who was informed of the speeches of this John Ball, caused him to be taken and put in prison for two or three months to chastise him.  Nevertheless, it would have been much better at the beginning that he had been condemned to perpetual prison or else to have died, rather than for the bishop to have allowed him to have been again delivered out of prison; but the bishop's conscience would not let him be killed. And when this John Ball was out of prison, he returned again to his error, as he did before.

 

Many people in London were informed of his words and deeds, people who had great envy toward those that were rich and noble; and then they began to speak amongst themselves and said how the kingdom of England was very evilly governed, and how gold and silver was taken from them by those who were named noblemen: so thus these unhappy men of London began to rebel and assembled themselves together, and sent word to the aforesaid counties that they should come to London and bring their people with them, promising them that they should find London open to receive them and the commons of the city to be of the same accord, saying that they would do so much to the king that there should not be one bondman in all England.

 

This promise moved so the people of Kent, Essex, Sussex, Bedford and of the counties about, that they rose and came towards London to the number of sixty thousand.  And they had a captain called Wat Tyler, and with him in company was Jack Straw and John Ball: these three were chief sovereign captains, but the head of all was Wat Tyler, and he was indeed a tiler of houses, an ungracious patron. When these unhappy men began thus to stir, the people of London, except those who were among the rebels, were greatly afraid. Then the mayor of London and the rich men of the city took counsel together, and when they saw the people thus coming on every side, they caused the gates of the city to be closed and would allow no man to enter into the city. But when they had thought well about it, they advised not so to do, for they thought they should thereby put their suburbs in great peril to be burnt; and so they opened again the city, and there entered in at the gates in some places a hundred, two hundred, by twenty and by thirty, and so when they came to London, they entered and lodged: and yet in truth the third part of these people could not tell what to ask or demand, but followed each other like beasts, as the shepherds did of old time, saying how they would go conquer the Holy Land, and at last all came to nothing. Likewise these villeins and poor people came to London, a hundred miles away, sixty miles, fifty miles, forty miles, and twenty miles away, and from all counties around London, but the most part came from the counties named above, and as they came they asked always for the king. The gentlemen of the counties, knights and squires, began to doubt, when they saw the people began to rebel; and though they were in doubt, it was good reason; for a less occasion they might have been afraid. So the gentlemen drew together as well as they might.
