Description

Although the Roman empire "fell" in western Europe, the eastern half of the Roman empire survived for over a thousand years, and is known to us as the Byzantine empire.  Until the thirteenth century, it remained one of the most powerful and splendid societies in the world, far overshadowing the emerging countries of western Europe.  This course is designed as an introduction to Byzantine history and civilization, A.D 330-1453.  In it we will explore the survival of the eastern empire, how it developed a distinctive Christian culture and ideology, how it interacted with, and impacted, its neighbors, and how it responded to economic, political, and military challenges. 


The 1000 years of Byzantine history consists of ups and downs; periods when strong emperors and generals brought peace, or at least victory, to the empire, and periods in which political or social turmoil and weak emperors led to internal chaos and disastrous losses of territory.  Ultimately, this is a story of the decline of an empire, but a decline that was not continuous.  One of the things we will consider is what stayed the same throughout the course of this 1000 years, and in what ways Byzantine society and culture changed radically.



Learning outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

- knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of the Byzantine Empire between 300 and 1500.

- facility in reading and understanding written primary sources

- the ability to deploy relevant evidence from primary sources, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, to construct historical arguments about life and culture in the past

- the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally



Course requirements

                7%       Attendance

              40%       Four 5-7 -page papers (10% each)

              10%      Participation in debate

              17%       Midterm exam

              26%       Final exam

           100%       TOTAL

Class meetings will consist of lecture and discussion.  Readings from the textbooks are assigned in order to provide background and supplementary material to what happens in class.  Discussion, group activities, debates, and other types of exercises will take place at various points in the semester, and you will be expected to be prepared for these.

Attendance will be taken in each class.  You will be allowed two 'free' absences.  If you are sick or have a valid emergency, let me know (email is fine) and your absence will be excused (i.e. not count as one of your two 'free' absences).

Descriptions of the papers, including the topic on which you must write, can be found here.  Each paper is based on one of the full-length primary source readings for the class.  You must read each book before you write your paper, as well as any relevant textbook readings.  Each paper must be turned in in class on the day that it is due.  Even an hour after class will be counted as late. Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (with no deadline, except the last day of classes). You do not have to use that option; remember that if a paper is late, it is not guaranteed to be graded before the next one is due.

On five days we will have a debate in which members from the class participate.  You will be assigned to a team of 3-4 students for one debate.  ASSIGNMENTS WILL BE MADE AT THE THIRD CLASS MEETING.  You are required to meet with your debate team at least once before the debate; the instructor will help to facilitate these meetings.  You will be graded individually on your participation, and each person will have ample opportunity to participate.  A website has been created for each debate, linked to the online syllabus, that contains the debate topic, readings (or links to readings), images, and other materials.  Further instructions for the debates can be found here.

The midterm and final exams will be a combination of short identifications and essays (they may also include map sections).  They will be open-note tests; you may NOT however use books or photocopies.  It behooves you, therefore, to attend class and take good notes; also to take good notes on readings and other materials.  Because this form of test is often difficult for people who haven't taken one before, a practice questions will be made available two weeks before the midterm. 

There will be opportunities for extra credit during the semester; usually this involves attending a lecture and writing a one-page summary of it.  These will be announced as the dates are publicized.  Note that attending a lecture or session (and writing a summary) will earn you 2% on your semester average; I will give each person a maximum of 4% extra credit earned in this fashion.  Summaries must be turned in one week after the event.
