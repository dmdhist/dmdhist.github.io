Some Guidelines for your Papers

A.  General information
I want you to learn from these papers that history is based on evidence, and that it is not permissible to make a statement about something in the past without basing it on some type of evidence from the time period.  Evidence can be written - documentary, literary, historical - or it can be material - archaeological remains, surviving buildings and works of art, etc.  All evidence requires interpretation, of course, and that is what I am asking you to do in your papers.  I am giving you a finite amount of evidence for each paper, and asking you to interpret only this evidence (and not any other) to produce a piece of historical writing.

For each of the papers, there is a choice of two topics; you may write on whichever you wish, and you do not have to write the same kind of paper every time.  For both topics, you are asked to synthesize information from the primary sources and the material remains to create one short piece of writing.

• The first topic asks you to respond as a historian to a question or questions, in a coherent short essay.  The questions are fairly straightforward; more details about the essays are given below.

• The second topic has you write from the point of view of an inhabitant or visitor to the community, in a more historical-fiction style, and has the potential for you to be quite creative. By all means be creative if you wish, but YOU WILL NOT BE GRADED ON CREATIVITY, BUT ON HOW WELL YOU CONSTRUCT HISTORY BASED ON THE SOURCES THAT HAVE BEEN PROVIDED! So your focus MUST be on the sources, and on writing history from the sources.  Let me emphasize again that if you choose to write on the second topic, EVERY ITEM OF INFORMATION IN YOUR PAPER MUST COME FROM ONE OF THE ASSIGNED SOURCES!  Do not assume anything!  THIS TOPIC IS HARDER THAN THE OTHER ONE!

To help you write your papers, a worksheet has been placed in Canvas Files, "H206 paper worksheet.docx." The worksheet is designed to help you assemble the pieces of information that you need for your paper.  You are strongly encouraged to fill out this worksheet for each paper that you write; once you have done this, you will have assembled most of the information that you need for the paper. The worksheet is not required, nor should you turn it in if you complete it; however, the Course Assistants are happy to look over your worksheet before your write the papers.

You have been assigned a variety of primary source readings in association with each unit (these can be accessed by the links on the online syllabus).  Some of the readings are about the specific communities we are discussing, but some are not.  In the latter case, the readings contain information about the society that is closely related in time and space to that community, and so you are to use it as evidence for what life might have been like in each community.

Your other sources are the material remains from each community, as presented in class and as found on the associated websites (linked from the online syllabus).

Before you start writing, think about the connections between primary sources, communities, and the historical context in relation to the question.  The connections are there -- you just need to find them to put it all together.   Next, underline the passages from the primary sources that you think will help you to answer the question and choose the images that you will use to support your points. Make a short outline.

Papers must be 750-1000 words long, and should be created in MSWord or a Word-readable format as a double-spaced paper.  DO NOT SUBMIT PDFs.  Papers must be uploaded to the correct Canvas Assignments item, by 1:00 pm (the start of class) on the day that they are due.

Papers are evaluated using Turnitin, against web sources and against papers from previous versions of this class. Do your own work!

It is intellectually dishonest, and against university rules, to copy information from a previous paper, from a book, or from a website, without citing your source -- this is called plagiarism. For more information, see https://wts.indiana.edu/writing-guides/plagiarism.html.  If you plagiarize, submit the same paper as someone else, or are otherwise academically dishonest, I am required this to handle according to University policies (http://studentcode.iu.edu/responsibilities/academic-misconduct.html), and you will fail that assignment. If you are in any doubt, you should cite your source.

If you need help with your writing, Writing Tutorial Services provides both group and individual instruction for free; I strongly encourage you to consult them early in the semester.


B.  Historical argument and evidence
One of the things that I want to emphasize is the importance of making an argument based on evidence. Evidence can be a quote from a text written at the time we are discussing, it can be a piece of material culture. Whenever you make an argument, or even a statement, you should be able to back it up with a specific piece of evidence.

When analyzing a piece of literature, you might provide a quote from the text and then interpret it ("this means ____"). In the type of historical writing that I want you to do, you should make the statement first, and then provide the evidence that backs it up.

So, for example, you should not make vague statements like "Women had low status in the Middle Ages." Instead, you should say, "Women had fewer rights and a lower value in early medieval society. For example, in the Salic Law, it says that stealing a male slave has a penalty of 1400 denarii, whereas stealing a female slave has a penalty of 1200 denarii." Thus, the statement you made has been backed up by a piece of evidence from a primary source. If you can provide two pieces of evidence for the same argument, that's even better!


C.  Requirements for the papers
In your papers, you must do the following five things.  Each of the following criteria is worth one letter grade.  If one of these is missing, you will be marked down a letter grade, if two are missing, you are down two letter grades, etc.

1) Answer the question.
Read the assigned question on the syllabus carefully. IF YOU CAN'T FIND THE QUESTION, ASK!  Most of the paper topics involve two or more related parts. When you are writing your paper, make sure you answer all parts of the question.

2) Historical context
In each paper, you should not be talking about "medieval times", but you should know something about the particular time and place that your paper is about:  6th century Italy, 12th century England, etc.  MAKE SURE THAT YOU HAVE A SPECIFIC DATE AND A GEOGRAPHICAL PLACE IN THE FIRST PARAGRAPH OF YOUR PAPER.

-  Look over your class notes and skim the assigned reading in the textbook to refresh your sense of the historical context. Note that the historical context should complement your analysis but should never be the focal point.
-  Describe the historical context in a sentence or two, e.g. "This village was occupied in Britain after the Roman government had pulled out; as a result, ______."
-  DO NOT provide a summary of the lectures associated with the paper. This is a common mistake. I do not want you to summarize the lectures at all; the lectures provide the background that will enable you to understand the primary source materials, but you do not need to repeat very much of that information.

3) Primary sources
You must use evidence from three of the assigned primary sources.  "Using evidence" in this case means deriving information directly from a source, quoting or paraphrasing from the source, and identifying that source in a footnote.  For example:

As we walk through the forum, we stop to watch a particularly exciting checkers game.1
_______________
Cassiodorus, Variae, Selection

There is no formal footnote format required; you may simply use the reference that is provided on the syllabus.

-  Read each source carefully. Ask yourself the following questions:
               1) What kind of document is it (A legal code? A letter? An inventory? A sermon?)              
               2) What is it about? Whose point of view is expressed in the document (is it
              biased toward one party or another? How?) What is the document's purpose?
               3) What kind of information can you find in it that will help you to answer the
              question?
*** Keep in mind that even if the document does not relate specifically to the community you are writing about, it may pertain to a  community similar to it. In this case it is acceptable to make comparisons.
-  Very long quotes should be avoided unless absolutely necessary. Only highlight the information that is relevant to your point and paraphrase the rest.  If you quote more than three lines, you should indent that quote and single-space it.

4) Specific physical remains from the community
You must use information about the physical remains from each community (Ravenna, West Stow, etc.) as evidence in your essay.   Look at both the Powerpoint presentations and the webpages linked to the syllabus for information about the community.  How does this information relate specifically to the question?  How can you use specific pieces of information about the community as evidence to answer the question?

-  One problem many students have is that they simply say "Wharram Percy was a feudal village."  This is not obvious; it has to be proven.  If you say "Wharram Percy had one or two manors that were obviously larger and more imposing, but most of the village consisted of peasant households; this corresponds to what we know about feudal social organization" you are on the right track. 
-  You must be specific.  Thus, don't just say something vague about how big and impressive Chartres cathedral is; say something specific about one part of it.  You have been provided with plenty of data; you are expected to use it.

5) Organization and style.
One of the criteria for grading will be a well-organized and well-written essay. 

-  Your paper MUST have a clear introduction. Do not simply rephrase the question; instead, provide a short overview of what you will cover in your paper. It is entirely permissible, even encouraged, to conclude your introductory paragraph with the statement "In this paper I will. . .".  Such a statement both helps you to figure out what you are doing in this paper, and helps the reader understand what you are doing throughout the paper.  Such a statement should probably be answering the assigned questions; if what you are doing in the paper doesn't answer the questions, you're not doing the right thing.
-  The body of your paper should support your points with primary sources, material covered in class, and historical context.
-  Grammar, spelling, and punctuation will all be part of this component of the grade.
