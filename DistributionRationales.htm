Distribution requirements - Rationales

The Current Requirements:
            Most students enter our program with an idea about what they want to study, and many leave it having done precisely that, whereas others find that their focus, or their ideas about history, have changed.  We want to allow for student exploration and especially for student creativity in thinking about their own programs of study. And yet, it seems advisable for students to learn at least one area of history in some depth, while also learning about more than one time and place, since experiencing the variety of history and the different approaches one may take to it is a great attractions of the discipline. A tension exists, therefore, between the desire to allow maximum space for student choice and guiding them in certain directions. The current distribution regime intervenes rather strongly to enforce both a specialization in one area (4 courses) and breadth of study of other areas (2 courses in something else, then 2 more in something else).
            Problems: the current requirements are cumbersome in prescribing that students take 8 courses in at least three different areas, even if we do leave it up to them to decide what those areas will be.  This choice means, however, that it is possible for a student to focus entirely on the 20th century, or on the non-western world, or on the pre-modern world; or they could avoid any kind of themed class (Jewish, Comparative, etc., not to mention Gender, which isn't even a category currently). It seems odd to make so many requirements of various areas of study and yet enable overspecializations of these kinds to remain possible. More practically, some students have difficulty meeting the concentration requirement, and others would like to concentrate in fields that currently don't exist, and still others find these requirements and the fields underpinning them confusing. The existing fields are, in fact, a hodge-podge of area, chronology, and thematic divisions, and do not match the department’s graduate fields or any other departmental grouping.

a. two classes before 1750, two classes after
 The date 1750 is chosen with an eye to the fact that the way history is done before that date is typically quite different (in terms of sources and methodologies) from the way that it is done after that date. In many fields before 1750 evidence is more scarce and may not be available in languages students can read except in translation; evidence may also not take a form with which students are familiar (such as archaeological evidence or artistic evidence). Historians may engage in more conjecture. The cultures students study may appear more alien. In the period after 1750 the opposite problem pertains in many parts of the world: there may be a surfeit of evidence, and students may assume a familiarity of culture that doesn’t actually exist. These very real differences in the way history tends to be done in earlier vs. later eras are something students should experience. Mandating chronological depth thus not only enjoins some variety in a students’ curriculum, but also serves a distinct pedagogical purpose. Purely geographical mandates (take so many courses about this continent vs. that continent, e.g. North America vs Europe vs Latin America/Africa/Asia) serves variety alone.
            It is worth noting that we currently teach a great many courses in a range of geographical areas on both sides of the 1750 divide.

b.  one pre-1750, one non-western, one modern western
Like proposal (a), but adding a provision of requiring western and non-western history while slightly diluting the degree of chronological experience. In this way students would be exposed to areas of the world they might not be inclined to study but which represent more than half the world’s population, while encouraging at least one course’s worth of chronological depth. The justification for requiring western/non-western work is not so much pedagogical (exposure to different ways of doing history) as it is about broadening horizons - students should have some experience of the history of peoples outside (and inside) the dominant American and European cultural milieu. <
            Importantly, like proposal (a), proposal (b) simplifies and loosens the distribution requirements.

c.  concentration of 3 classes, secondary field of 2
This will loosen requirements somewhat, reducing some of the practical problems associated with our current distribution system by cutting the number of courses students have to worry about coming in this or that field. It would thus help make way for any new (non-distributional) course requirements we may wish to add to our major as part of this review process. However, it leaves in place (and indeed worsens) the possibility for overspecialization, whether in terms of one era of history or one type of history. 

d.  2 courses in each of 3 fields
By requiring three different fields of coursework, this proposal serves to enforce variety in this same way as our current system, but takes away the 4-course concentration in one area. Thus this will simplify things a bit, perhaps paving the way for more requirements in other areas of our program, though not as much as proposals (a) – (c). For those who like the idea of aggressively promoting the study of a variety of areas without picking out this or that era or area of the world for special attention, this proposal may be the best.

e.  status quo, removing elective requirement
Another minor tweaking of the current system, giving students a bit more ability to specialize in two areas, and lessening the enforced variety of the current program. Like proposal (d), this will simplify things slightly, though not as much as the other proposals.
