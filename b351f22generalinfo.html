Description
The Early Middle Ages (c. 500-1000 AD) was a time of dramatic cultural, political, and social change. During these five hundred years, Europe experienced invasion, conversion, and other upheavals that overturned the old Roman imperial order and shaped entirely new systems. By 1000, Europe contained many of the political, cultural, religious, ethnic, and linguistic boundaries that we know today, and thus the Early Middle Ages can be regarded as the period in which the foundations of modern western society were put into place. It was a time of pronounced intercultural conflict and accomodation - not focused on racial differences, but certainly including ethnic, religious, political, linguistic, and class distinctions, which were themselves constantly in flux.

We will begin by briefly considering the "fall of Rome," and will then look at the political, cultural, and religious institutions that arose in the wake of the dissolution of a dominant European power. We will end around the year 1050, because after 1050 Europe again experienced a large number of new trends and transformations. Throughout the semester, we will be examining the different ways that Roman, Germanic, Christian, and Islamic traditions interacted to produce a new Europe.

Descriptions of the papers, including the topic on which you must write, can be found here. Each paper is based on one of the book-length primary source readings for the class. Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (up to two weeks after the original due date). The midterm and final exams will be in-class essay exams. They will be open-note tests; you may use any materials that you like. It behooves you, therefore, to come to the exams with good notes, whether based on class meetings, readings, or other materials. Because this form of test is often difficult for people who haven't taken one before, a study guide will be made available two weeks before the midterm.

Learning Outcomes
A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:
- knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, religions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of western Europe between 500 and 1000.
- awareness that social, cultural, and political trends are related to each other, and discussion of how, for example, theological disputes shaped and were shaped by their social and political contexts
- knowledge of some of the major artistic, architectural, and literary trends throughout this period, and how they were related to political and social stimuli
- facility in reading and understanding written primary sources in a variety of genres (diplomatic correspondence, legal code, charters, personal correspondence, historiography)
- the ability to deploy relevant evidence from primary sources, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, to construct historical arguments about life and culture in the past
- the ability to present historical information and make historical arguments orally
- the ability to talk about historical subjects with people who have not studied them
