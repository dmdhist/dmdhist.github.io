Description

The Roman empire has enjoyed mixed reviews since it ceased to exist. In modern popular culture, the Roman empire is usually depicted as an authoritarian, repressive, decadent, violent, and basically uptight institution; Edward Gibbon in the eighteenth century thought it was, at its height, a humane and enlightened political entity.  The truth, of course, is somewhere in between. Nevertheless, everyone recognizes that it covered a large and very diverse territory, it lasted for a long time, and then it "fell".


The phrase "the decline and fall of the Roman empire", coined by Gibbon, evokes images of decadence, violence, and abrupt political and social upheaval.  Ever since the "fall", people have been trying to make sense of why it fell, and whether its fall was a good or a bad thing; there are four shelves in the library with books on the "fall of the Roman empire".  Some modern scholars, however, prefer to speak of "the transformation of the Roman world", emphasizing that the changes that took place in the late Roman empire were not necessarily symptomatic of 'decline', but rather of profound religious, social, and political transformations.


This course, however, will focus on questions of what "decline" and "fall" mean, and how we can evaluate these concepts.  We will examine the Roman Empire, its neighbors, and its successors from the third through the seventh centuries A.D., and we will consider the political, cultural, intellectual, and religious factors that made the Roman empire what it was, and what happened to them in the wake of events such as the "barbarian invasions".  Particular attention will be paid to what the people of the time thought was happening to the empire in which they lived, as seen through their writings.



Course requirements

The following are the requirements for this course:


Attendance                                                    8%

Participation                                                  10%

Four 4-6-page papers (15% each)                  60%

Final essay exam                                            22%


Attendance will be taken in each class. You will be allowed two 'free' absences. If you are sick or have a valid emergency, let me know (email is fine) and your absence will be excused (i.e. will not count as one of your two 'free' absences).


Participation:  five classes are specifically designated as discussion or debate (Aug. 26, Aug. 31, Oct. 7, Nov. 4, and Nov. 18).  These have readings specifically assigned for them.  You are expected to have done the readings for these classes, and to participate actively in the class activities.  A very brief reading response question has been assigned based on each reading, which will be graded 2 (good), 1 (minimal), 0 (unacceptable), and will factor into the participation grade.


General instructions for the papers may be found here.  Each paper is based on all of the readings, both primary and secondary, for the given unit. Because we will discuss the papers in class, they are due at the beginning of class on the day that they are due.  Late papers will be marked down one letter grade per day that they are late; however, you will get one "free pass" to turn in a paper late (with no deadline other than the last day of classes).  You do not have to use that option; remember that if a paper is late, it is not guaranteed to be graded before the next one is due.


The final exam will be an open-note essay test, and in particular will rely on the papers you have already written.  You may use any notes, books, photocpies, or online resources. Since you won't know the questions ahead of time, the best way to prepare is to know the material: attend class and take good notes, take good notes on readings and other materials, and make notes of your notes.


There will be opportunities for extra credit during the semester; usually this involves attending a lecture and writing a one-page summary of it.  These will be announced as the dates are publicized.  Note that attending a lecture or session (and writing a summary) will earn you 2% on your semester average; I will give each person a maximum of 4% extra credit earned in this fashion.  Summaries must be turned in one week after the event.

Learning outcomes

A successful student will demonstrate, by the end of the semester:

• knowledge of key events, peoples, institutions, religions, and ideas that shaped the history and cultures of western Europe from the third to the seventh centuries AD, related to the phenomenon known as the "fall of Rome.".

• understanding that the interpretation of a political/cultural/religous changes is dependent on the aims and interests of the interpreter

• facility in reading and understanding written primary sources in a variety of genres

• the ability to deploy relevant evidence from primary and secondary sources, in an effective and persuasive manner in writing, to construct historical arguments about life and culture in the past
